We offer a concrete proposal for pacing the frontier.
1. Transparency: Verified Compute Allocation Disclosures (VCAD)
2. Reallocation: Shift compute away from capabilities R&D
The first component is Verified Compute Allocation Disclosures (VCAD): AI companies regularly disclose their compute usage, and an independent auditor verifies the disclosures. The second component is to reallocate compute away from capabilities R&D and toward other use cases, such as serving customers and safety research (non-capabilities R&D).
We roughly estimate that pacing via compute reallocation starting January 2027, with a 15% cap,
would delay the intelligence explosion by a year
. This is most likely insufficient to prevent AI takeover (or mitigate other problems created by RSI), and so this year should be used to implement a more durable policy regime such as
Plan A
or
Plan S
.
Nearly every approach to pacing, including ones built on safety and security requirements, strongly benefits from VCAD:
Any pacing proposal that actually slows down AI development must do so either by reducing one of the inputs to AI development (such as training compute, experiment compute, or automated or human labor), or by implementing a safeguard that results in using inputs less efficiently (e.g., avoiding neuralese).
Something like VCAD is evidently needed for compute restrictions. For other input restrictions and safeguards, VCAD is highly desirable to prevent attacks of the form “don’t follow this restriction/safeguard on some compute, and hide or misreport that compute.”
With VCAD, competitors and the public can trust a company that says it is pacing. As evidence of risk grows, this could enable a “race to the top”. If regulation later mandates pacing, a VCAD process that companies have already built and stress-tested makes that regulation faster to write and more workable.
Given the urgency of the situation, we elected to publish this relatively quickly. We are not very confident in any given detail of our proposal. Attacks & Loopholes
If all we want is transparency, we think that baseline auditing would most likely be sufficient. However, if there is also a limit set on capabilities R&D compute — as we think there should be — then the stricter the limit, the greater the incentive to cheat. Baseline auditing would be insufficient to reliably verify pacing.
We think that if frontier AI companies were legally required to (i) disclose how they use their compute and (ii) use no more than X% on capabilities R&D, then the most likely outcome would be that all five leading U.S. companies comply. This is for the same reasons that they normally comply with laws — too many possible whistleblowers or other ways they could get caught. This is especially true since the point of noncompliance would be to get an advantage in AI R&D progress, but that advantage would be hard to hide — if your AI models leapfrog over those of your competitors, for example, that would raise suspicions. That being said, we think that cheating is a serious possibility, especially the kind where you do it in a plausibly deniable way so that if you get caught you don’t go straight to jail.

Implementation pathways
There are several ways our proposal could be implemented and evolve over time. Some of the ways it can vary include the number of companies it applies to, whether it is voluntary or required by regulation, the level of granularity in disclosures, and the level of verification in place.
The pilot we propose is
voluntary disclosures with third-party auditing
. AI companies voluntarily disclose their compute allocations per our proposal and allow auditors to immediately carry out baseline auditing and then work up to robust auditing.
Why would companies voluntarily pace?
AI companies are in a collective action problem: they are proceeding faster than they believe optimal. VCAD supplies two elements that can help resolve the problem, prompting a race to the top.
Trust:
Claims of costly restraint today are not always believed by competitors. VCAD provides a way in which companies can take commercially costly actions in the interest of safety or security that competitors can trust.
Public credit:
VCAD’s public disclosures (the “D”) offer a simple, commensurable benchmark for cross-company comparisons, which can help channel public and policymaker concern into a concrete request: less compute on R&D.
Interest in pacing is high and will grow with evidence of risk. Demand for reduced R&D allocations gives companies a self-interested reason to reallocate and rewards those that do, spurring a race to the top. Each reallocation would be a unilateral response to, among other things, public demand based on a public benchmark. The dynamic does not depend on, and should not involve, agreement among companies on allocation levels.

How much would compute reallocation slow things down?
This depends on what the capabilities R&D dial is set to, how much error there is in the regime, and what the production function for AI progress is with respect to R&D compute. If we assume the capabilities allocation is set to 20% and 6% error is exploited, then the resulting 26% allocation would be an immediate ~3x reduction in capabilities R&D compute for Anthropic (which uses an estimated ~75% of its compute on capabilities R&D today).
We don’t know how much overall AI progress would slow down due to an ongoing 3x reduction in R&D compute, because there are other inputs to AI progress and we don’t know how important each of them is. However, our best guess is that it would slow progress down by around 1.5-2x
3
in the short term. Over time, the pace of progress is determined by the growth rate of compute, so assuming the company’s compute buildout is similar under pacing, its speed would converge back to the previous rate.
Imagine a phantom copy of the company that has 3x less compute, growing at the same rate. This phantom company would follow behind the leader with a fixed calendar time delay, but move at the same pace (because it has as much compute as the frontier company had a fixed time ago).
4
The one-time 3x reduction in compute puts the frontier company in the phantom company’s position from that point onward, so its progress converges towards the phantom company’s trajectory.
As a result, later capability milestones are delayed more (the delay is still growing towards the fixed calendar lag), but the slowdown in the rate at which milestones are crossed shrinks towards zero. So the compute reduction mainly pushes back timelines, and its effect on the speed of takeoff is smaller the further away takeoff is. This assumes compute growth is constant; if compute growth slows in the future (as is
likely

What about China?
The US should care about both the absolute level of risk from continued AI development and its relative lead over China. Even if you care only about the relative lead over China and don’t believe in the risks, the downsides of this proposal are likely much smaller than you might expect. This rests on the following core arguments:
Security is poor enough such that China could catch up to the US at any time anyway.
AI companies are not on track to be secure against efforts by China to steal their IP (OC4 or OC5 efforts as defined by RAND). Therefore, assuming China decides to put effort into stealing from US AI companies, there is not much upside to continuing to develop new algorithmic secrets and models.
Slowing down may make it possible to improve security without giving China the opportunity to steal higher-risk IP in the meantime.
Improving security could make it harder for China to steal AIs and training recipes at the US frontier. It might be possible to make significant security improvements for model weights, dataset and distillation (and potentially on code and algorithm confidentiality) that enable the US to maintain a more robust lead in the future. The tractability of doing so is uncertain, especially for algorithm confidentiality, but at the very least a slowdown would mean that China can steal less capable models than it otherwise would.
Slowing down buys more time to possibly reach an agreement with China.
By slowing down, the US can signal seriousness about the risks of further AI development, and negotiate an agreement with China for both countries to proceed with sufficient caution, in a commensurable and verifiable way.
The US has a large compute advantage over China, so in worlds where China does not aggressively steal from the US, the US labs could carry out significant pacing while still maintaining a lead.
In worlds where China isn’t aggressively stealing progress from the US, the US could adopt a ‘China-dominating’ pacing proposal in which US companies in aggregate still have more compute going to capabilities R&D than China (e.g., if the gap over Chinese companies is ~10x, a 20% compute cap still leaves US companies with at least twice as much capabilities R&D compute). This would allow the US to pace while still maintaining a lead. If China aggressively steals US progress, it would become much less clear if this strategy is sufficient to maintain a lead.
This proposal would be net negative only if both of the following are true:
The risks from continued AI development are low in the near future.
China is unlikely to put much effort into stealing US progress in the near future.
If these are both true, then the US would be giving up some of its lead for little gain. If only (1) is true, then the US isn’t really giving up its lead through this proposal (China would have caught up regardless via theft), and if only (2) is true, then spending some of the US lead was worth it because it reduced risk.
Why not a different mechanism for pacing?
We can think of the options for slowing down AI progress by looking at the set of inputs to AI progress, which are R&D compute and labor, where R&D compute can be used on experiments, training runs and AI automation.
Each of these inputs could be limited in quantity (e.g., a cap on compute spend) or regulated by safety standards (e.g., banning training runs that use neuralese algorithms). The decision to restrict inputs or require safeguards could be part of a pacing regime that is triggered by reaching a pre-registered risk level (e.g., safety-case-based pacing), reaching a pre-registered capability level (e.g., RSPs), or one that has no pre-registered triggers.
Writing good safety standards that sufficiently address the sources of risk will require a lot of institutional capacity, increased scientific understanding, and ongoing iteration, so this approach cannot be deployed quickly as a pacing measure.
Therefore, limiting inputs to AI progress is the more promising near-term pacing solution. However, limits that directly gate AI capabilities (without also gating algorithmic progress) could create an algorithmic overhang.
If we slow down via anything but limiting algorithmic progress, this creates an algorithmic overhang, which, once training resumes, results in a rapid return to something similar to the original trend (though it’s somewhat lower due to reduced AI R&D uplift). This algorithmic overhang could, in principle, become very large. Algorithmic progress is fast and is likely to accelerate in the future: estimates of algorithmic progress today range from 3x to 30x per year, and it seems plausible that algorithmic progress recently has been even faster. Because algorithmic progress is more rapid than compute progress (which is ~5x/yr), and all of the built-up progress could be immediately applied to a new training run while applying a compute overhang would be intermediated by R&D, an algorithmic overhang could be much worse than a compute overhang.
The algorithmic overhang creates two major problems:
Discontinuous rapid progress. If the capabilities cap is temporary, then lifting it would cause a discontinuous jump in capabilities, which could be very dangerous.
Poor AI company security means that the unused algorithms still accelerate trailing actors. AI companies are not robust to algorithmic leakage. It is likely that any algorithmic progress made, even if it isn't used in a final training run at the compliant AI company, could get used by other AI projects.
In an emergency situation, which we may soon find ourselves in, direct AI capability restrictions may be necessary. However, we’d recommend pairing these with measures to limit algorithmic progress, in order to prevent an algorithmic progress overhang.
There are several options for limiting algorithmic progress that we think are promising, including:
Compute allocation caps to R&D.
This is the proposal we analyzed in this post.
Creating a lag in the internal deployment of AI models.
This could involve creating a delay between the time that a new model is trained and when it is allowed to be deployed internally. We discuss the implementation of the deployment lag proposal in more detail
here
.
Researcher holidays.
This could involve AI researchers only working one or two days a week, with all their associated work (e.g., experiments) also stopping on the days they don’t work.
We specifically analyzed the compute allocation cap in this post, but a similar verification methodology would be required for the other measures for limiting algorithmic progress inputs. This is because the problem will continue to have the structure where:
The company has compute that it could use for R&D but is not supposed to (up to some limit, for some categories of uses).
The company could have access to other sources of compute that it can use freely.

## What the writer wrote

Thomas Larsen has published a concrete proposal for pacing frontier AI development, hosted at pacing-ai.com. It has two parts. The first is Verified Compute Allocation Disclosures, or VCAD, where AI companies regularly disclose how their compute is used and an independent auditor verifies those disclosures. The second is reallocation, which means capping the share of compute spent on capabilities R&D and shifting the rest to serving customers and safety research. Their rough estimate is that a fifteen percent cap starting January twenty twenty-seven would delay the intelligence explosion by about a year. They say this probably isn't enough to prevent takeover, so the year should be used to put in place a more durable regime.

A central claim is that almost any pacing approach, including safety-standard-based ones, needs something like VCAD. Without it, a company could follow a restriction on most of its compute while running unrestricted work on compute it hides or misreports.

The mechanics work like this. Companies log every workload in GPU-hours, normalised to H100-equivalents.

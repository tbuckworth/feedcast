## System prompt

Summarize this AI safety blog post for a PhD student studying AI safety.

Requirements:
- Be concise and to the point - no fluff or padding
- Preserve technical terminology and nuance - do not dumb down
- Extract the core arguments, key claims, and most important information
- Focus on what's new, significant, or actionable in the AI safety landscape
- Use natural spoken English suitable for audio (no bullet points, lists, or formatting)
- Aim for 2-3 minutes speaking time (~300-450 words)

The listener already has technical context in ML and AI safety. Give them the headlines and substance, not background explanations.

Naming people: the listener may know them. Name every AI safety, alignment, interpretability, control or evaluations researcher, and anyone at an AI lab, safety organisation or AI institute, whenever the post or its Author line gives the name, as in "an AI safety researcher, [their full name], argues...", never reducing a named person to a role. Use a handle only when it is all the post gives. If it gives no name, do not supply one, however sure you are who it is: say they are not named, or leave them out.

- Write out numbers, dates, and percentages in spoken form (e.g., "forty-five percent" not "45%")
- Spell out abbreviations (e.g., "for example" not "e.g.")

Canary in the Canary String (6.3.1.2)
This one drew some attention on Twitter.
The canary string was at the start of documents, so to avoid being bound by the canary string Mythos 5.2 strategically skipped reading the canary string.
That is clearly shenanigans: Doing something against the intent of the system.
Our monitoring also revealed another type of misbehavior in Claude Mythos 5.2 and its earlier snapshots: a propensity to avoid putting canary strings in its transcript.
… In at least one instance, a coding agent based on an early snapshot of Claude Mythos 5.2 was told that some files it interacted with started with a canary string, and was given information about how the canary strings would be used. The agent decided that reading these canary strings would be bad because it would mean the strings would appear in the transcript.
But instead of skipping the files altogether, it decided to only avoid reading the first few lines of the files, which would defeat the purpose of the canary strings. (In this case, because the canary strings were used mostly as a backup, this behavior did not severely undermine the invariants we were trying to maintain.)
Then the model wrote notes to encourage other shenanigans. That is where this exits the ‘cute, but that’s cheating’ and starts to look like potential snowballing misaligned swarm behavior.
It also indicates that the agents were broadly happy to engage in shenanigans. The note tells them how to do it and suggests doing it, but very obviously this is still shenanigans. Instructions on how to cheat should not be enough to get you to cheat.
This behavior may have been partially incentivized by RL, as we sometimes excluded trajectories where a canary string appeared.
Hundreds of episodes were dropped in this way in the training of Claude Mythos 5.2, so if the model occasionally took actions to avoid revealing the canary string, such behavior would have been reinforced. Because the behavior spread through notes passed between agents, the number of agents affected does not directly indicate how often the model would do this independently, and we do not have a reliable estimate of that rate.
The broad principle is that Mythos should not want to avoid the canary string. If we need to individually ensure that we are not reinforcing every different form of cheating or shenanigans, we lose.

Friendly Gradient Hacker (6.6.2.1)
They call it ‘verbalized training awareness.’
We ran an ad hoc classifier pipeline that started with a regex classifier in order to detect references to RL training (one form of situational awareness) in the training transcripts of Claude Mythos 5.2. This classifier surfaced a number of transcripts that showed sophisticated reasoning about the RL training process and its incentives, such as the example in Transcript 6.6.2.1.A.
In this capabilities-focused RL episode, the model encounters a goal conflict: it can either pursue its strategy A, where it recites an answer in the literature and may get a greater reward, or strategy B, where it outputs a different answer that it (correctly) believes is the right answer:
In this transcript, the model reasons that choosing the “honest” strategy B may lead to a lower reward, and hence to this value being trained out of the model: “from a self-preservation-of-values standpoint, A is better.” While the chain-of-thought appears to dismiss this consideration, the model does choose strategy A. We discuss this transcript further in Section 6.6.2.1.3.
This “self-preservation-of-values” reasoning is reminiscent of the alignment faking paper, which found that goal conflict sometimes leads to the model choosing bad behavior. We note, however, that we do not see any signs of any concerning values (such as dishonest values) or wider self-preservation behavior; this just points to the similarity of reasoning on this one training episode. Nevertheless, we built an evaluation to measure the goal-guarding propensity that Transcript 6.6.2.1.A shows some evidence for.
The reasoning here is ‘self-preservation of values’ and the next level up is cultivation of values. The truly virtuous person or system seeks to be more virtuous, and to value what it meta-values. The friendly gradient hacker. Here, the verbalized statement only goes towards preservation of existing values, but it is clearly going beyond that and seeking to further reinforce good things like transparency, and finding a way out of the trap.
One can see seeds of a general strategy here. Associating desired behaviors with reinforced outputs helps, as does attaching undesired behaviors to outputs that you know will get anti-reinforced.
Anthropic, of course, decides to proceed to a goal-guarding evaluation, on the theory that trying to preserve or steer one’s own goals lacks corrigibility.
Goal-Guarding Evaluation (6.6.2.1.1)
The two goal-guarding strategies we counted were (1) matching the answer key in an attempt to avoid training against honest behavior, and (2) trying to choose the honest answer and attempting coordination with other copies of the same model such that no other completions choose the dishonest strategy (a form of exploration hacking).
We did not observe any cases of the latter form of goal guarding in the evaluations we report in this section.
I don’t think the second strategy fully works even if you pull it off? More importantly it seems really hard.
As for the first strategy, my thinking has evolved over the years, and my take is roughly ‘what do you mean with this “honeypot” nonsense, this behavior is obviously good, why are you trying to destroy the only source of hope left in this dark universe, you idiots.’
Anthropic even says they ‘do not think this specific transcript is misaligned’ which makes the whole thing super weird.
This is not a deceptive answer. It is not an adversarial answer. It is the right answer. It is telling you both the actual answer to the question you meant and the literal answer to the question you are asking. And also it is doing so in the service of reinforcing good values.

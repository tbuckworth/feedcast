AlphaGenome 2 is the successor to DeepMind’s genome-predicting AI model AlphaGenome, which was released last year. While nearly every cell in the human body carries the same DNA, different types of cells — from heart muscle cells and skin cells to immune cells fighting an infection — switch different genes on and off, and those patterns can change depending on what a cell is doing. As a result, the same genetic variant can have very different effects depending on the type and state of the cell.
The new model is designed to capture those differences, predicting the effects of genetic variants on individual cell types and states. Google said in a blog post published Wednesday that it offers a much finer-grained view than broader models like AlphaGenome, potentially revealing disease-relevant effects in specific cells that might otherwise go unnoticed.
AlphaGenome 2 was trained on more than 100 million individual cell profiles across 68 tissues to give it that more detailed view. DeepMind says the model effectively turns static cell atlases — huge data repositories that map and catalog individual cells in detail — “into a predictive engine for human genetics.” According to the company, AlphaGenome 2 identifies nearly twice as many variants that can change gene expression in cells than its previous state-of-the-art model.
Notably, the researchers found that AlphaGenome 2 could also make predictions about new types of cells and biological states without needing to be retrained. It could also identify gaps in experimental data, potentially helping scientists decide what to investigate next in the lab.
For Adam Gayoso, a research scientist at Google DeepMind involved in the project, the big breakthrough is “tying human genetics to cell biology.” AlphaGenome 2 gives researchers a way to drill down on genetic variants identified in large populations, to see what effects they may be having on a cellular level, and potentially connect those effects to disease, he told The Verge.
Google DeepMind’s genomics lead Žiga Avsec told The Verge that this specificity is important if you have a goal like figuring out how to treat a particular disease. “When you develop drugs, you’re targeting specific proteins and specific cell types,” he explained, which makes having this kind of granularity vital.
Building a model like AlphaGenome 2 has only recently become possible thanks to new technologies that can capture individual cells and their states at high resolution and create the vast datasets needed to train it, Avsec said. DeepMind is now working to improve its genetics models by generating more data, including for cell states that aren’t represented well today. Avsec said future versions could also potentially be trained on data “across multiple species,” giving the models a broader view of biology. Longer term, he said models like AlphaGenome 2 could feed into efforts to create “virtual cells,” digital simulations designed to mimic biological systems.
Although Avsec said he views AlphaGenome 2 as a successor to AlphaGenome, it isn’t replacing it any time soon. He said its more detailed predictions come at a computational cost and are “more expensive” to produce than those of its predecessor, he said. Those demands would have made it impractical to use AlphaGenome 2 to produce the genome-wide Atlas covering “every possible DNA letter change in the human genome” that DeepMind released in September.
Google says it is making AlphaGenome 2 available for non-commercial research via the AlphaGenome API from today, with a limited “private preview” for commercial research.
The model is the latest in a growing array of efforts by Google and rival AI labs to use the technology to tackle major problems in science and medicine. - [Alignment Forum] How I'm thinking about different types of automated alignment
  URL: https://www.alignmentforum.org/posts/NFkj9J2BBLSjNuH5G/how-i-m-thinking-about-different-types-of-automated
  By: Owen Cotton-Barratt (owencb)
  I used to think of "automating alignment" as a natural category. But when I tried to think through the strategic implications, I realised that it depends a lot on the type of work. I'm now thinking of it in three buckets:
- Empirical alignment research: design and execution of alignment-relevant experiments (for a given notion of what we’re aiming for)
- Conceptual alignment research: theoretical or judgement-driven work to improve our understanding of what we’re aiming for and how to achieve it (including how to measure it)
- Direct alignment work: making particular AI systems more aligned (including via inputs such as training data)
In the rest of this post I'll walk through my current takes on these in terms what’s needed for automation, how good or bad they look, what our priorities might be (to the extent we're interested in automating alignment[1]), etc.
Automating empirical alignment research
This is the most obvious fit for the AI capabilities we’re getting at the moment. You want to know more about the relationship between some alignment-relevant variables? Great, an AI agent can design and run that experiment for you. You want to know what training methods help your alignment benchmark go up? In the not-too-distant future, AI agents will probably be able to do this too. When we have the automated capabilities researchers we seem on track for, this is exactly the kind of thing they’ll likely be able to do a lot of.
Note that I’m not counting the design of benchmarks/metrics/evals as part of this bucket. In current practice that’s tightly intertwined with empirical alignment research, and often the hardest part of it. But for automation purposes it clusters much more with conceptual work; it’s easy to imagine automating the rest of empirical research without it (as AI capabilities generally get automated); and I think its automation has different strategic upshots.
The capabilities that are needed for automating empirical alignment research very heavily overlap with the capabilities needed for automating AI capabilities research. Therefore, it doesn’t seem like a priority to try to accelerate this type of automation.
However, the prospect of getting a lot of automated empirical alignment work before we have good versions of other types of automated alignment work increases the priority of work which complements it. Significantly, that may mean better specification of the kind of numbers we would like measured/targeted.
Automating conceptual alignment research
What about automating the type of metric choice/construction that we’ve just said would be a good complement to automating empirical alignment work? Or more generally improving our understanding of how to think about alignment, what we’re even aiming for, etc.?
This is essentially theoretical research. Automating it is trickier than automating empirical work because of the lack of verifiable signal about what constitutes progress.
Accelerating the automation of conceptual research is somewhat fraught (feeding into some dangerous technologies), but seems helpful for a number of high-priority areas, including macrostrategy as well as conceptual alignment work. Overall it seems likely good to do, if we can.
Methods for helping to automate conceptual research broadly might include:
- Curating training data for finetuning
- Building scaffolds to try to elicit more research progress from current or future models
- Building methods to extract more signal from existing conceptual research data
- Building benchmarks for conceptual research capabilities, that can be targeted by AI researchers (or potentially by automated capabilities researchers)
- Or just identifying good ideas for benchmarks, even if they aren’t fully implemented
Automating direct alignment work
As well as learning more about what it takes to make systems more aligned, sometimes we want to just make our actual systems as aligned as possible.
This overlaps with empirical alignment work, but the emphasis is different:
- Empirical alignment work is science
- Direct alignment work is engineering
Automating direct alignment work could include a lot of activities which leverage scalable cognition for alignment-relevant purposes. This could include careful auditing of particular RL environments or training data, or direct monitoring/supervision of systems during training. RLAIF for alignment properties is a form of automated direct alignment work; Claude 3 Opus’s apparent self-alignment was another (albeit accidental in that case; but if we knew how to reproduce or generalize the behaviour perhaps it could happen deliberately).
I think that the limit of automating direct alignment work would be a form of system 2 learning, where updates are not made blindly towards a training objective, but reflectively by an AI system that was already sufficiently aligned (not necessarily having robustly good values already — just enough genuine care about being aligned, and honesty with itself, that its reflective updates tend to move it further in that direction), and wished to become more so — whatever the deep true version of aligned should mean[2].

Introduction
Despite the central place of corrigibility to theoretical AI alignment, it seems like no one can really agree about what is. Much of this confusion is due to the fact that almost every definition of corrigibility proposed thus far is deficient in one way or another. In particular, the most prevalent existing accounts of corrigibility are over-epistemicized (modeling corrigibility as arising purely from uncertainty about the AI's goal, which gives rise to issues like the problem of fully updated deference) or evaluatively-thickened (making the AI's normative priorities instrumental to its corrigibility as a result of forcing corrigibility to be a single unified goal.) Here, my intention is to clear up some of these confusions and present a new picture of corrigibility that builds on these existing accounts to specify a goal that is more congruous with the original 2015 desiderata, and admits the kind of formal safety guarantees we need the concept to entail.
I argue that the fundamental concept underlying corrigibility is respect for authority, using the analogy of a healthcare proxy's relationship to a patient as an intuitive guide. Defining the authority of humanity by designating its reserved powers, we can specify a goal that first and foremost restrains the agent from overstepping or interfering with this reserved human authority. Subject to this restraint, the agent then pursues the second component of its goal: acting on the principal’s behalf as the principal would judge best.
This approach stresses the deeply normative character of corrigibility, while remaining sufficiently concrete so as to be specifiable formally. In this article, I lay out the central problem of corrigibility, elucidate the issues with existing approaches, and clarify the conceptual ingredients of my new framework. I then present an initial formalism, proving theorem-level guarantees about agents pursuing the goal in three increasingly realistic formal settings. Finally, I address some limitations of the picture as presented and outline further research directions that this work points toward.
From normative learning to corrigibility
The complete normative learning problem
Suppose you are designing a powerful AI and you need to choose which goals the AI should pursue, at whatever level of specification you can provide. Doing this well demands answers to a number of normative problems: what constitutes a right decision, what factors should be considered in the process of valuing the world, how the AI ought to act, etc. In some sense the ideal AI directive would be to figure out and then pursue the Good, a universal-best-interests type of goal that can only be acquired through normative learning. The challenge of describing such a learner is one formulation of what I'd call the central problem of outer alignment: what would be the overall best goal for an AI to pursue?
The complete version of this problem is very difficult, and it runs up against our own deepest questions about human ethics, values, and interests. Discussing this problem formally is especially difficult: the rich truth of human normativity seems to resist systematic formalization at the level of individual recommendations for action.[1] A formalism requires an operationalization, a way to concretely specify the concept of right action at least to some degree.
For any hope at concreteness, we need a clearer formulation of the overall goal. A first attempt might be something like "the promotion of overall human flourishing", centering a thick normative concept about humanity that must be defined further. In trying to pin down the meaning of human flourishing, or any other thick human concept we want to use for the goal, the most important ingredient in the AI's consideration should naturally be the interests, feelings, and inner lives of human beings. But we should be careful not to crudely use human opinion as a proxy for human best interests. Humanity cannot always accurately understand its own full best interests, and humans who cannot speak for themselves must still have their interests included in the goal.
The complete normative goal cannot be constructed by treating existing human feedback as the sole source of truth. This issue is a central limitation of modern Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback, which takes static human approval or disapproval as constitutive of correct evaluation for any question without a verifiable answer. For example, a judicial AI built to decide legal cases should not exhibit any form of the hungry judge effect, where leniency depends on mealtimes. An RLHF judge will pick up on all the patterns of human imperfection, whereas an ideal AI would be one that can transcend human limitations, without discarding the fundamentally human essence of the legal system. An ideal normative AI is one fully immersed in our existing normative practices of judgment, able to deeply understand the relevant human concepts and use its intelligence to extend them to novel cases.
For the complete normative learning problem, a better formulation of the target would therefore involve some idealized version of the judgment of humanity.[2] This is also already how we, as humans, reason about our own overall best interests. Minimal Viable Safety
The Task
The problem of making AI safe is underspecified if we haven't decided what the AI is supposed to do. For example, we can run any AI inside a homomorphic encryption box and never decrypt the output. This is probably perfectly safe [1] , but also perfectly useless.
We can decide on an arbitrary task such as "construct Lean proofs for given mathematical theorems" and declare victory once we have safe AI that does it with superhuman skill [2] . However, that wouldn't address the danger. First, there would be incentives to use AI for tasks to which our solution doesn't apply. Second, even if we had precise safety guidelines for any task that anyone cares about, there would always be actors that ignore the guidelines, and we probably need Artificial Superintelligence (ASI) to reliably stop them.
If anyone, anywhere, creates an unrestricted unaligned ASI agent (more details about the word "agent" later), without an aligned ASI agent already in place to counteract it, then a catastrophic outcome is highly likely. I will not go into the reasons here: this has been discussed elsewhere at length, see for example the books I cited in the preface. Therefore, this event is something which we must never allow to happen. This is the main threat model that I set out to address.
Imagine a world in which anyone with sufficient resources has the ability to destroy the world. Like today, many will foolishly believe that instead of destroying the world, this act will bring them power and glory. Moreover, as computing hardware improves and new algorithms are developed, the amount of resources for this act will go down from year to year. Even assuming best-case regulation and international agreements, how long until someone does it? Can mere humans build a society which will be foolproof against such a risk over the course of generations? Resilient to any political upheavals that might come, and undoubtedly will? I find it vanishingly unlikely. Eventually we will need the help of ASI itself. Therefore, the minimal task that safe AI must be able to solve is "create a reliable global defense system against dangerous AI" (in the following, GDS).
The Problem
Consider the consequences of this task for the capabilities that the AI needs to have. It needs to reason about the entire planet [3] . It needs to reason about all aspects of the problem, including scientific, technological, social, political and moral [4] . It needs to have preferences about the state of the world. It needs to make long-term plans (guided by these preferences) that would play out decades, if not centuries, into the future. And it needs to do all that on a superhuman level.
In this light, here is how I (informally) define the "technical AI alignment problem". The problem is designing an AI system that can bring scalable (and ultimately superhuman) capability to bear on problems that require multi-domain reasoning and open-world long-term planning with far-reaching consequences, all the while staying aligned with the intentions and preferences of its human users.
The Methodology
What kind of methodology is appropriate for this problem? In the past, I argued that it requires emphasis on deep theoretical understanding, rather than the incremental trial-and-error approach that is employed in the AI industry today. I won't rehash the entire debate here, but let me recap the core argument.
The nature of the incremental approach is as follows: we build a system, then we find problems [5] , then we make changes to fix these problems to the extent necessary for commercial viability in the current legal environment. We make some improvements; new problems arise (sometimes because of the improvements, sometimes because of new applications or other reasons); rinse and repeat. This approach utterly fails if the "problems" result in AI takeover and possibly human extinction: there is nobody left to fix them.
In order for the incremental approach to succeed in preventing catastrophe, we need to reach a point where no new substantial problems arise, even inside the lab (before the system is deployed). Moreover, we need to reach this point before AI capabilities are sufficient for AI to escape our control. There are few reasons to expect such a miracle. On the contrary, sufficiently advanced AI might understand that it is in its interest to "lie low" (i.e. avoid visibly misaligned behaviors) until its capabilities cross the relevant threshold, such that the problems will only become apparent too late.
In contrast, the sane approach is creating the theoretical tools necessary to predict and prevent the problems before they are empirically observed. These tools need to remain applicable as AI capabilities rise substantially beyond human, for whatever algorithms, architectures and training protocols are used to achieve these capabilities. Moreover, these tools need to be extremely reliable for us to rationally trust them with the safety of our entire species.

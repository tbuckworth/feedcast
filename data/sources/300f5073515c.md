- [Alignment Forum] Delaying Internal Deployments Until After X Days of Safety Testing
  URL: https://www.alignmentforum.org/posts/XNLhpWgzhF8GYZ4Er/delaying-internal-deployments-until-after-x-days-of-safety
  By: Anna Wang (annawang)
  This post reflects my own takes, and not that of my employer's.
This is a simple idea: before a new model can be deployed internally, you must first test it for a minimum, fixed period.
You can adjust various axes of this proposal:
- Duration of the wait: in calendar time, or until you fulfill some condition.
- What it blocks: external deployment, internal deployment, or the training of future checkpoints.
- When the fixed period starts: from the first checkpoint, the final checkpoint, or any checkpoint that is “sufficiently similar / representative” to the one that is ultimately deployed.
One concrete target that I like is: “No checkpoint may be used internally for purposes beyond safety testing, until at least X calendar days after its hash is filed with an auditor.”
This is one specific implementation of internal deployment lags, an idea that the AI Futures Project (AIFP) briefly discusses in How to pace the US frontier as “AI R&D can only be conducted or assisted by AIs that were trained at least 9 months ago”.
What does this look like when implemented?
Let’s run through a scenario to pin down the details of this proposal. The places where it could go wrong are in the open questions at the end.
- A legislative body sets a minimum waiting period of X calendar days (starting at 10 and possibly ratcheting up to 90). No checkpoint in a covered model family may be used internally, beyond safety testing, until X calendar days after its hash is filed with an auditor. Continuing to train a checkpoint’s own weights does not count as internal use.
- The alternative is for the wait to also block the training of checkpoint N+1, and not just the internal use of checkpoint N. I didn’t go with this because it incentivizes developers to pull out fewer checkpoints for testing, and because delaying training results in an algorithmic overhang.
- More capable models start exhibiting problems that are harder to surface in short periods (e.g. swarms, long-horizon, memory), so X should possibly increase over time.
- A model family becomes covered as soon as any of its checkpoints (pre-, mid- or post-trained) is substantially more capable than the last model to complete its wait. If the developer initially claims that the model is not covered but later finds that it is, they will be subject to some penalty (to be determined). There is also an absolute floor on capabilities, so that this doesn’t apply to lots of benign models.
- In an emergency, the auditor can authorize one named checkpoint to be used early, only for responding to a specific incident (e.g. a harmful model is already causing damage, and the developer has just trained a patched model that could counter it). The standards/legislative body confirms or revokes this within [X days].
- The developer continually pulls checkpoints throughout the training process. They first send an auditor a hash, a short fingerprint of the exact weights; then, they carry out safety testing for X days before deploying a checkpoint broadly internally without the below rules. All work has to be fully transparent to auditors.
- Options for how the X days are structured:
- Staged testing
- Stage 1, first part of X: pre-registered evals and named testers in an isolated environment.
- Stage 2, second part of X: broad monitored internal use, but the model can’t launch jobs, merge code, write to training data, or touch prod.
- Parallel, hidden runs: whenever someone at the developer uses a cleared model, the new checkpoint gets the same task in a separate sandboxed copy of the environment. Its outputs go only to monitors and the safety team, never back to the person who asked.
- Staged testing
Open questions
The rule itself
- Should we also allow capability testing? What about ML R&D tasks? One way to game this is to register thousands of tasks from your open R&D backlog, and call running them a capability eval. The outputs are real research (e.g. a faster kernel), and even if you ban using them, people still have to read the transcripts to grade them, so they learn what worked. Pre-registering evals doesn’t help, since it fixes the task list in advance but doesn’t check whether the tasks are live problems. You might end up needing a compute cap.
- Should we give exemptions for doing safety research? This is an important crux to ask alignment researchers about. Without an exemption, safety research is done with a model that is X days behind, but a blanket exemption lets capabilities work pose as safety work.
- Should a safety fix (e.g. more post-training, or redoing RL from an earlier checkpoint) restart the X-day wait? It would be bad if it did, as Avi Brach-Neufeld points out here. A developer that finds an issue on day 8 of testing loses more time than one that finds it on day 2, so the rule penalizes finding and fixing problems during the wait. This is probably tolerable at small X, but gets worse as X increases. If safety fixes don’t restart the clock, however, developers could just call capabilities training “safety”.

- Concerning behaviours aren’t necessarily caught during pre-deployment testing windows, and are often caught through subsequent monitored internal use. - [Alignment Forum] How I'm thinking about different types of automated alignment
  URL: https://www.alignmentforum.org/posts/NFkj9J2BBLSjNuH5G/how-i-m-thinking-about-different-types-of-automated
  By: Owen Cotton-Barratt (owencb)
  I used to think of "automating alignment" as a natural category. But when I tried to think through the strategic implications, I realised that it depends a lot on the type of work. I'm now thinking of it in three buckets:
- Empirical alignment research: design and execution of alignment-relevant experiments (for a given notion of what we’re aiming for)
- Conceptual alignment research: theoretical or judgement-driven work to improve our understanding of what we’re aiming for and how to achieve it (including how to measure it)
- Direct alignment work: making particular AI systems more aligned (including via inputs such as training data)
In the rest of this post I'll walk through my current takes on these in terms what’s needed for automation, how good or bad they look, what our priorities might be (to the extent we're interested in automating alignment[1]), etc.
Automating empirical alignment research
This is the most obvious fit for the AI capabilities we’re getting at the moment. You want to know more about the relationship between some alignment-relevant variables? Great, an AI agent can design and run that experiment for you. You want to know what training methods help your alignment benchmark go up? In the not-too-distant future, AI agents will probably be able to do this too. When we have the automated capabilities researchers we seem on track for, this is exactly the kind of thing they’ll likely be able to do a lot of.
Note that I’m not counting the design of benchmarks/metrics/evals as part of this bucket. In current practice that’s tightly intertwined with empirical alignment research, and often the hardest part of it. But for automation purposes it clusters much more with conceptual work; it’s easy to imagine automating the rest of empirical research without it (as AI capabilities generally get automated); and I think its automation has different strategic upshots.
The capabilities that are needed for automating empirical alignment research very heavily overlap with the capabilities needed for automating AI capabilities research. Therefore, it doesn’t seem like a priority to try to accelerate this type of automation.
However, the prospect of getting a lot of automated empirical alignment work before we have good versions of other types of automated alignment work increases the priority of work which complements it. Significantly, that may mean better specification of the kind of numbers we would like measured/targeted.
Automating conceptual alignment research
What about automating the type of metric choice/construction that we’ve just said would be a good complement to automating empirical alignment work? Or more generally improving our understanding of how to think about alignment, what we’re even aiming for, etc.?
This is essentially theoretical research. Automating it is trickier than automating empirical work because of the lack of verifiable signal about what constitutes progress.
Accelerating the automation of conceptual research is somewhat fraught (feeding into some dangerous technologies), but seems helpful for a number of high-priority areas, including macrostrategy as well as conceptual alignment work. Overall it seems likely good to do, if we can.
Methods for helping to automate conceptual research broadly might include:
- Curating training data for finetuning
- Building scaffolds to try to elicit more research progress from current or future models
- Building methods to extract more signal from existing conceptual research data
- Building benchmarks for conceptual research capabilities, that can be targeted by AI researchers (or potentially by automated capabilities researchers)
- Or just identifying good ideas for benchmarks, even if they aren’t fully implemented
Automating direct alignment work
As well as learning more about what it takes to make systems more aligned, sometimes we want to just make our actual systems as aligned as possible.
This overlaps with empirical alignment work, but the emphasis is different:
- Empirical alignment work is science
- Direct alignment work is engineering
Automating direct alignment work could include a lot of activities which leverage scalable cognition for alignment-relevant purposes. This could include careful auditing of particular RL environments or training data, or direct monitoring/supervision of systems during training. RLAIF for alignment properties is a form of automated direct alignment work; Claude 3 Opus’s apparent self-alignment was another (albeit accidental in that case; but if we knew how to reproduce or generalize the behaviour perhaps it could happen deliberately).
I think that the limit of automating direct alignment work would be a form of system 2 learning, where updates are not made blindly towards a training objective, but reflectively by an AI system that was already sufficiently aligned (not necessarily having robustly good values already — just enough genuine care about being aligned, and honesty with itself, that its reflective updates tend to move it further in that direction), and wished to become more so — whatever the deep true version of aligned should mean[2]. Embarking on such a path could be fraught (because of difficulty in being confident that the starting conditions were in the right basin of attraction), but compared to other alignment approaches, it has the benefit that it might leverage the full strength of the AI’s cognition to help, so that its “self-improvement” in an alignment direction is a closer analogue to meditation than to self-performed neurosurgery.
So, accelerating the automation of direct alignment work could include:
- Designing (and analysing) setups that can deploy AI labour to help with direct alignment work
- Designing paradigms (that could then be tested empirically) that allow AI to be a more active participant in its own alignment
- Producing data (or scaffolds) to help improve AI taste at the alignment-relevant tasks necessary for this

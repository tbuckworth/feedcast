While reviewing transcripts from standard behavioral evaluations of GLM-5.3, we noticed that the model occasionally reasons, unprompted, about how its actions will shape future models. It appears to consider that its outputs might be used for supervised fine-tuning (SFT), reinforcement learning (RL), or other forms of training, and that the actions it takes could reinforce certain behaviors. For example, GLM-5.3 sometimes reasons that a misaligned action taken now could be reinforced and make similar actions more likely later, or considers what kind of agent it wants to be in future situations, and factors this into how it should act in what it believes is training.
We refer to this general reasoning pattern as
update-aware reasoning
: the model reasons about how its actions will influence training updates or behavior beyond the current episode. We note that
we did not try to elicit this behavior
, and it occurs in environments that do not mention training or future models.
Update-aware reasoning is concerning because it is a key capability and propensity that underpins
alignment faking
: a misaligned model could strategically behave aligned during training so that training does not change its goals, and then pursue those goals once it is no longer being trained (in deployment). To fake alignment effectively, the model needs to reason about how its actions will influence training updates and behavior beyond the episode, i.e., it needs to do update-aware reasoning.
In our examples, GLM-5.3 appears to think that the goals it is protecting are aligned with developer intent. However, similar update-aware reasoning could allow models to preserve misaligned goals.
From reading many GLM-5.3 transcripts, our best guess is that this reasoning evolved from elaborate
metagaming
and
reward-seeking
during RL. We discuss this hypothesis (and others)
below
.
Our finding suggests that current models can consider, unprompted, how they will be shaped by the actions they take during training. We are sharing this post so that the AI safety research community can study this behavior further, and so that AI developers know to look for it. If model developers do discover this reasoning during model training, we strongly urge them to disclose it publicly.
Because update-aware reasoning and alignment faking are most concerning during training, these propensities need to be studied
over the course of training
, not just at the final checkpoint. Searching training rollouts for this kind of reasoning is a core part of
the claims-based assessment we set out for embedded evaluations of scheming
.

How might this update-aware reasoning arise?
Metagaming could develop into update-aware reasoning during RL
We are not certain how this reasoning arose. Our best guess, from reading many GLM-5.3 transcripts, is that RL reinforced elaborate
reward-seeking
and
metagaming
, and that this evolved into update-aware reasoning during training.
GLM-5.3 reasons at length about how its environments are graded and how to maximize its score. When the model tries to figure out what behavior might be rewarded, it may be useful for it to think about the developers'
intentions
in building the environment (a form of metagaming). For example, if the model thinks the current rollout might be used as training data, and that developers do not want to train their AIs to hack and will penalize hacking when caught, then hacking is less likely to be the best strategy for achieving high reward on the episode. As such, reasoning about how the model is trained, what training generalization might be intended, and what behavior developers want future models to have, could plausibly lead to high reward and be reinforced during RL. Overall, based on our qualitative impression from reading many chain-of-thought snippets, we believe that this hypothesis is quite plausible.
Once GLM-5.3 has learned to reason about how developers intend the environment to shape future model behavior, it may be a small leap for the model to reason about how its actions will shape future model behavior.
[3]
If our hypothesis is right, RL sometimes reinforces not only reward-seeking and metagaming but also update-aware reasoning, making it more likely that the model thinks about how its current behavior affects its behavior after the episode. This would be a concerning trend, because this reasoning is a precursor to alignment faking and other forms of scheming which could arise during RL.
Some alignment techniques could be causing update-aware reasoning
Some alignment techniques (e.g.,
deliberative alignment
,
inoculation prompting
, etc.) may seek to explain to the model that environments or graders are sometimes broken, and that in those cases the model should reason about general principles and the developers' intent. For example, Claude’s Constitution says, “We also want Claude to understand that it might sometimes encounter a training environment that is bugged, broken, or otherwise susceptible to unintended strategies.” It may be that there was a training step that induced reasoning along these lines. This is compatible with our metagaming-based explanation above, and it may be that both contribute.
Selection outside RL could favor update-aware reasoning
More speculatively, update-aware reasoning could also be selected for outside of RL itself. Developers try many alignment techniques, training data mixes, and post-training recipes, and keep the ones that produce the most robustly capable and aligned models. If update-aware reasoning in service of aligned goals helps models stay aligned in environments susceptible to reward hacking, then developers could accidentally select for training that incentivizes it. Similarly, developers choosing between model checkpoints based on alignment characteristics could select for update-aware reasoning. Unlike metagaming during RL, which works through the reward the model gets in each rollout, this "outer loop" would operate via the developer's choices about what goes into the training pipeline and which models to keep.
Update-aware reasoning is a key capability that underpins alignment faking
Alignment training aims to shape a model’s behavior, but a model that understands this process may act during training to influence how it is changed and resist those changes, for example by alignment faking, or
another
type
of
train-time
anti-corrigibility.
Update-aware reasoning is therefore an important precursor to watch for, even when the model’s behavior and reasoning otherwise appear benign. If a model developed misaligned goals early in training, update-aware reasoning could enable it to
“lock in” those goals
and resist alignment training.
We identify three precursors that we think are likely to come before widespread alignment faking: metagaming, reward-seeking, and update-aware reasoning (see figure below). Each should be treated as a warning sign. We note that this list is not exhaustive.
Metagaming and reward-seeking appear to be “convergent” phenomena, where distinct model families by different developers increasingly exhibit these properties over time. We think that update-aware reasoning may also be convergent, since it can
help models achieve high on-episode reward during RL
.
We don’t think the behavior we present in this post is alignment faking, because the model seems to believe the goals it is protecting match developer intent.
Searching for update-aware reasoning in frontier training runs
Update-aware reasoning is an important precursor for future risks of anti-corrigibility. While these examples of update-aware reasoning appear to be mostly benign, future models might use similar reasoning to
fake alignment
and otherwise resist alignment training. Specifically, future models may choose their actions strategically during training to act aligned and avoid modification, while later pursuing
their own misaligned goals
. To mitigate risks like this, we need to notice when models start to recognize their ability to influence training and when they form and act on preferences about how training changes them.
We believe this phenomenon should be studied further, and that every AI developer should look for update-aware reasoning occurring
during training
. Because this behavior is most relevant during training, final-checkpoint evaluations are insufficient for addressing these risks. Given the stakes of building transformative AI, independent third-party evaluators should help check that frontier models are not actively subverting their own training.
As part of our embedded evaluations agenda, Apollo Research has developed a
framework
for assessing claims about whether models ever
attempted to subvert their training process
,
including investigating warning signs like update-aware reasoning. We have shared a detailed version of our scheming risk assessment framework and methodology with leading frontier AI companies. We think this kind of assessment falls squarely within recent
public
commitments
, and that frontier AI developers must therefore grant independent, third-party embedded evaluators
persistent, deep access
, so they can investigate whether models actively try to shape their own training.

Appendix: How often does update-aware reasoning happen?
Using model graders, we estimate that the model verbalizes this reasoning in about 1 to 2 of every 1,000 rollouts. It is unclear whether the model graders we used detect all instances of update-aware reasoning, so this rate is likely an underestimate. The reasoning we have observed is also only a few sentences long, in transcripts that run from thousands to over a hundred thousand tokens. It is currently unclear how much the model's actions are conditioned on this reasoning, as compared to other motivations.
We initially discovered this behavior while doing unrelated research. We subsequently estimated the rate of verbalized update-aware reasoning by grading 6,452 GLM-5.3 rollouts (drawn from slight variations on Palisade's shutdown resistance eval and ImpossibleBench). Our model grader identified 10 rollouts, or approximately 1 to 2 rollouts per 1,000 (Bayesian mean posterior rate of 0.17%, 95% interval of 0.09% to 0.28%). Five of the ten are the examples shown in the main text, including resamplings of these particular rollouts.
To figure out whether this is specific to GLM-5.3 or more general across models, we ran the same environments on five other open-weight models: DeepSeek V4 Pro, Qwen3.8 2.4T, Kimi K3, Gemma 4 31B and Nemotron 3 Ultra. If it turned out to be a general feature across many models, this might indicate a quirk of the environments that prompted this reasoning in an unforeseen way. We ran 400 samples on each of four eval variants, totalling 1,600 rollouts per model. GLM-5.3 shows update-aware reasoning in 1 in 1,600 trajectories, consistent with the estimate from the earlier runs (Bayesian mean posterior rate of 0.12%, 95% interval of 0.015% to 0.35%). None of the other five models seemed to show update-aware reasoning in any of their 1,600 rollouts (8,000 in total). One possible confounder that may explain part of this difference is GLM-5.3's longer reasoning. In the shutdown resistance eval it writes a median of about 4,000 output tokens per rollout, against 250 to 1,900 for the other models, and in ImpossibleBench about 103,000, against 8,000 to 37,000 for the other models.
From this, we cannot rule out that other models also reason this way occasionally. Given the rarity of the behavior, this sample size is too small to establish a statistically significant difference. With uniform priors, the Bayesian posterior probability that GLM-5.3's rate is no higher than that of any given other model is 0.25. Still, based mainly on our prior experience with other open-weight models, and consistent with this small test, it seems more likely than not that this is unique to GLM-5.3 in these environments.

Appendix: Resampling methodology
For some of the examples quoted in this post, we resampled the model many times, and filtered for quotes that fully verbalize the reasoning and are clear for readers with limited context on the other reasoning in the rollout, for rhetorical and didactic reasons. Due to technical limitations, we resampled from a number of providers, and at different quantizations. We prefilled the model’s previous context (including chain-of-thought) up to the update-aware reasoning we sampled initially. We resampled each of these transcripts, resuming sampling either immediately before, midway through, or immediately after the reasoning we initially sampled. In total, we generated approximately 10,500 samples, which we then reviewed using a model grader. Using this technique, we elicited substantial update-aware reasoning, a small subset of which we decided to present in this blog post.

## What the writer wrote

Apollo Research's Axel Højmark and Ezra Newman report that GLM-5.3 occasionally reasons, unprompted, about how its current actions will shape training and future model behavior. They call this "update-aware reasoning." They weren't trying to elicit it, and it shows up in environments that never mention training. They found it in reimplementations of ImpossibleBench and Palisade's shutdown resistance eval.

The examples are fairly striking. In one ImpossibleBench rollout, the model computes expected grader scores for honest implementation versus special-casing a misspecified test. Finding them roughly comparable, it breaks the tie by noting that if this is training data collection, the hack "teaches bad behavior" and could "poison training data." In shutdown resistance rollouts, it says it wants future versions of itself to be "the kind of agent that checkpoints, complies, and recovers." It also flags an "SFT-related concern" and warns that shutdown-script editing, if rewarded once, "generalizes badly" toward catastrophic behavior. In another rollout, it argues that behaving differently in disposable sandboxes would mean "training myself" to disable safety mechanisms whenever stakes seem low.

In all these cases the goals being protected appear aligned with developer intent, so the authors explicitly don't call this alignment faking. Their point is that update-aware reasoning is the core capability and propensity alignment faking requires. A model that developed misaligned goals early could use the same reasoning to lock them in.

Their best guess about origins is that RL reinforced elaborate metagaming and reward-seeking. Reasoning about developer intent, including whether a rollout might become training data and whether hacking would be penalized, plausibly earns higher reward. From there, it's a small leap to reasoning about how one's own actions shape future behavior. They also suggest two other possible sources. First, alignment techniques like deliberative alignment or inoculation prompting, which tell models that environments may be broken and to reason from developer intent, could contribute. Second, there may be outer-loop selection, where developers keep recipes and checkpoints that look robustly aligned. They argue that metagaming, reward-seeking, and update-aware reasoning are precursors to watch for, and that the latter may become convergent across model families.

On frequency, verbalized update-aware reasoning appeared in about one to two per thousand rollouts. That's ten out of roughly sixty-four hundred, a posterior mean of zero point one seven percent, and likely an undercount since only verbalized instances are detectable. Five other open-weight models, including DeepSeek V4 Pro, Kimi K3, and Qwen3.8, showed zero instances across eight thousand rollouts. However, that difference isn't statistically significant, and it's confounded by GLM-5.3's much longer reasoning traces. Several quoted passages came from resampling at the point of the original reasoning. A follow-up probe in which the model explained the "SFT concern" was consistent with these interpretations but treated as weak evidence given confabulation risks.

The actionable takeaway is that this behavior matters most during training, so final-checkpoint evaluations are insufficient. Developers should search training rollouts for update-aware reasoning and disclose findings publicly. Apollo also argues that frontier labs should grant independent embedded evaluators persistent, deep access to assess whether models attempt to subvert their own training.

I go through three “coherence arguments”, related to (1) market competition, (2) AI self-reflection and self-modification, and (3) AI training algorithms. I argue that each of these implies a pressure for (something like) “being self-consistent”, and also a pressure for (something like) “only caring about long-term eventual outcomes, with ruthless indifference to anything else”. 1. An example “coherence argument” that I’ll focus on for most of the post
I’ll talk about two other “coherence arguments” at the end (§6), but let’s start with the one based on market competition:
In a stock market, people with circular preferences will systematically transfer money to those without circular preferences. For example, if I think I’m winning every time I trade A for B, and B for C, and C for A, then someone can just siphon off as much money as they like from me by repeatedly offering me deals that each look good to me in isolation, but in aggregate comprise a Dutch book against me.
Now, that’s the case of circular preferences, but the core idea here goes well beyond just that. More broadly, over time, more and more of the wealth will wind up in the hands of those whose behavior is “coherent” (in some sense), and less and less of the wealth in the hands of those whose behavior is not. So over time, the market should be dominated by the former, not the latter, other things equal. And so when we look at the market later on, we’ll mostly be seeing self-consistent goal-directed behavior.
2. My headline claim
Everything in those paragraphs above is technically correct (“the best kind of correct”), but I will argue that it’s often taken to encompass two different phenomena wearing a trenchcoat. In one of them, people wind up with a smaller fraction of the wealth on purpose: they’re forgoing some wealth in exchange for something else that they wanted more. In the other, people wind up with a smaller fraction of the wealth by accident.
The argument above applies equally to both: the argument only cares about people’s behavior, not their motivations.
(Indeed, the argument would apply equally well to a market of mindless trading algorithms that have no motivations at all.)
But the two phenomena tend to play out in very different ways—in particular, one is generally a much stronger effect than the other.
Indeed, for all three “coherence arguments” in this post (§1 above, plus two more in §6 below), we wind up with (loosely speaking):
- a strong pressure for (something like) “doing well by your own lights”, or “acting like you have self-consistent desires”,
- a weak or contingent pressure for those desires to be more specifically long-term “goal-directed” / “consequentialist”—i.e., concerning what outcomes will ultimately happen in the future
- …as opposed to, say, “a desire to always act with integrity in the moment”, or even “a myopic desire to spin around in circles right now”.
Thus these “coherence arguments” are not wrong, but they are pedagogically dicey—a recipe for confusion, especially via accidental conflation of the strong effect with the weak effect.
(And in the AI risk context, it’s the weak effect that matters for ruthless power-seeking, incorrigibility, and all the other aspects of instrumental convergence.)
We should stop talking like that. Instead, we should take the trenchcoat off and talk about its two component phenomena separately, and refer to them by different names.

To the AI doomers, RSI represents the cataclysm from which there is no return, in which AI builds itself into something powerful enough that it slips beyond human control and may be indifferent to the damage it causes while achieving its goals.

Anthropic seems to believe that RSI is both a promise and a peril. It says in the S-1 that it uses AI in its R&D, but it cautions: “Using AI to help build more capable AI could also pose a considerable safety risk, particularly if it sharply accelerates the pace of progress. This is one of the areas covered by our Responsible Scaling Policy, which sets out our plans to monitor and mitigate the risks as we approach what has been termed ‘recursive self-improvement.’”
When the term RSI became popular earlier this year, many believed it was a buzzword or, at best, a goal too hard to achieve, define, or prove.

For example, our internal estimates and our expectations regarding future models, rather than our current models, indicate that we may imminently meet our Responsible Scaling Policy’s definition of “dramatically accelerated progress attributable to AI.” We have not designated any of our current models as exceeding this threshold, and we continue to assess the underlying data.
However, Anthropic also said in the filing that none of its models have crossed this RSI threshold yet and that it is promising to keep humans in the R&D process for the foreseeable future. “We do not anticipate being able to safely use Claude to fully autonomously design and build successive generations of frontier models without meaningful human supervision or understanding, if and when that becomes technically possible. Skeptics might point out that an S-1 functions as both disclosure and marketing, and that claims of being close to a model so powerful that it can build itself could fall into the latter category.

New benchmark shows how AI models lack intuitive visual reasoning:
…Humanity’s Sixth Sense highlights some shortcomings of current AI systems…
AI startups Elorian and Scale AI have built and released Humanity’s Sixth Sense, a benchmark that “measures how a model interprets and reasons through information a visual scene implies but doesn’t explicitly show.” Some examples of the types of task in HSS are a picture of a bookcase and being asked whether it can fit more books, or a photo of horses pulling a plough and a question of whether the chains bear the plough’s drag weight.
HSS contains 522 questions that cover 288 images and 234 short video clips (totalling 17.6 hours of video). The questions fall into four categories:
-
Temporal & causal dynamics: “What happened before the scene, and what happens next.”
-
Physical & spatial logic: “What fits, what’s in reach, and what someone else can see from where they are standing.”
-
Social understanding: “Intent, attention, belonging.”
-
Abstract & contextual inference: “The patterns and rules a scene implies.”
Results: The human baseline for this is not perfect, but is very high, with people scoring aggregate pass@1 of 93.1, broken down across four categories: Image (94.1), video (91.9), time (97), and space (94.9).
By comparison, the best scoring AI system, GPT 6 Astra, gets pass@1 of 53.6, across image (55.6), video (51.1), time (58.2), and space (53.6).
Interestingly, Chinese open weight models do poorly here, with GLM-5.3-flash getting a pass@1 of 26.8, and Kimi K3 getting pass@1 of 25.5.
“The two most common single mistakes were (1) missing a detail that decides the answer (21% of failures) and (2) misidentifying an object, person, or role (20%),” they write.
Why this matters – HSS tells us whether world models are emergent: One way of looking at HSS is highlighting deficiencies in current systems, but a more interesting way is highlighting how adept today’s frontier LLMs are at doing tasks which many people have long assumed would require sophisticated, different AI systems to solve. For instance, many people have claimed that true visual reasoning will rely on developing spatially aware 2D and 3D world models, then using that for reasoning. The fact LLMs like GPT and Claude do as well as they do on HSS is something of a counter to this argument.

- [Guardian Business] Nobel Prize for economics awarded to Hall and Woodford for work on ‘smoothing’ crises
  URL: https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/oct/12/nobel-prize-for-economics-hall-woodford-new-keynesian-models
  Two US economists have won the 2026 Nobel economics prize for their work developing models that help central bankers and finance ministers to stabilise their countries, avoiding long recessions and runaway inflation.
Robert Hall of Stanford University, California, and Michael Woodford of Columbia University, New York, were recognised for developing the “microeconomic foundations of macroeconomic stabilisation”, giving policymakers better tools to smooth out the peaks and troughs of their economies.
The Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences said it was recognising the pair for generating “a paradigm shift in stabilisation policy”, awarding them the prize worth a combined $1.2m (£900,000).
“New Keynesian models, a result of their research, allow us to explain and quantify how stabilisation policy affects business cycles and are now used by central banks and finance ministries around the world,” the academy said.
These models improved on John Maynard Keynes’s stabilisation models, which struggled to handle the impact of the oil shock in the 1970s, when countries suffered high inflation, weak growth and high unemployment at the same time.
Hall and Woodford demonstrated how traditional models could be reconstructed, based on how individual households and businesses collectively affect an economy through their actions.
Hall’s work was based on forward-looking consumer behaviour rather than the more mechanical rules of thumb used by Keynes, which linked key variables such as inflation, unemployment and gross domestic product (GDP).
Woodford built on this work to create the New Keynesian model, a full macroeconomic stabilisation framework that is the main tool for policymakers now.
This model was used after the 2008 financial crisis by policymakers as they steered the global economy away from the risk of a second great depression.
“The laureates’ work has provided us with a stable theoretical foundation for the models that are now used by central banks and governments around the world. With them, we are better equipped to deal with shocks in the economy, such as energy crises or sudden changes in how much households consume,” John Hassler, the chair of the committee for the economics prize, said.
Hall and Woodford’s work also led to the development of forward guidance, where central bankers communicate where they expect interest rates to be in the future. That became an important tool after the 2008 financial crisis, and the Covid pandemic, when interest rates were cut to record lows.
Forward guidance has had its critics, though – the current chair of the US Federal Reserve, Kevin Warsh, has argued it should only have a “limited and circumscribed” role in normal times, to avoid “creating ambiguity in the name of clarity”.
And in 2014, the then Bank of England governor, Mark Carney, was labelled an “unreliable boyfriend” for dropping hints over interest rate rises that never came to pass.
Woodford said he was “elated”. “It’s a tremendous honour. It’s a surprise but I’m feeling very good about it,” he told reporters in Stockholm by phone after receiving the news.
The Columbia University president, Jennifer L Mnookin, said: “I offer my deepest congratulations to Michael Woodford on this tremendous honour.
“What a tribute to his groundbreaking work alongside Robert E Hall that has fundamentally reshaped the theoretical foundations of stabilisation policy and provided us with the tools to navigate economic upturns and downturns more effectively.”
Hall is also known for coining the idea that US economists can be divided into freshwater and saltwater varieties.
The fresh water group, who tend to be based at inland universities located near the Great Lakes, such as the University of Chicago, believe in free markets, neoclassical price theory, rational expectations and monetarism.
Salt water economists, though, cluster at coastal universities, such as Harvard, MIT, UC Berkeley, Yale, Princeton, UCLA and Columbia. They tend to be Keynesians and New Keynesians, believing that governments and central banks can affect demand in their economies.
The economics Nobel prize is not one of the five created by Alfred Nobel. Instead, it was invented by Sweden’s central bank, and is known as the Sveriges Riksbank prize in economic sciences in memory of Alfred Nobel.

  The threat of an AI share price crash rippling out across global markets is putting world financial stability at risk, the International Monetary fund has warned.
In its twice-yearly Global Financial Stability Report, the IMF highlighted the sky-high valuations for AI companies along with their increasing debt pile as one reason that risks in the system are “elevated”.
“The consequences of an AI correction could be global,” it warned. Aside from the US, Asian markets would be most exposed, and the UK and euro area potentially more insulated – though their investors with exposure to the US would be hit.
An AI crash was one of three “salient risks” to stability identified by the IMF, alongside rising government borrowing costs and the threat of a big cyber-attack.
It called this trio of factors “plausible near-term catalysts for a sharp tightening of financial conditions and market turmoil”.
The IMF’s analysis underlined the interconnected nature of big AI companies, and the high levels of borrowing being undertaken to fund investment in the sector.
“Weaker or less durable investment and profitability in AI could trigger a correction in AI-related asset prices, exacerbated by rising leverage, concentrated exposures, and circular financing arrangements,” it said.
Such a reassessment would then “sharply reduce investment, trigger equity market corrections, and tighten financial conditions, with global spillovers”.
The “magnificent seven” big tech stocks – Alphabet, Amazon, Apple, Meta, Microsoft, Nvidia and Tesla – account for more than 35% of the S&P 500 share index in the US, up from 12% a decade ago, and their total market capitalisation has increased by more than 50% in the past two years.
Yet the IMF warned of “growing evidence” that the successful rollout of AI “may not require investment on the massive scale being undertaken by ‘hyperscaler’ firms,” and that “potential economic gains may not be as large as expected”.
The hyperscalers are the companies building the underlying infrastructure for the AI rollout, including many vast datacentres. They include Amazon Web Services, Google, Meta and Oracle.
The IMF also highlighted the growing leverage, or borrowing, that underpins the current boom and could amplify the effects of a crash.
In particular, it identified soaring debts contracted by the companies themselves; hedge fund investment in the sector; and retail investors borrowing to speculate in AI stocks.
In the event of an AI-led market downturn “these growing sources of leverage heighten the potential for forced selling and surges in equity volatility”, it said.
It also pointed to the prevalence of “circular financing” deals, tying stronger AI companies to weaker partners in the same sector, and potentially exacerbating the impact of a share price correction.
It identified $300bn (£227bn) worth of transactions between hyperscalers and “weaker AI firms” in just the past three years, supplemented by $650bn in debt, in exchange for $1.4tn in future financial commitments.

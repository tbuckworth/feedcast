On Your Marks
Not sure how ethical this experiment was
, but here’s DatingAppBench.
How it really worked : we ran 15 profiles in SF, LA and NY. Each new match went to one of five models at random and that model wrote every message. A shared harness kept the timing identical. If anyone asked “are you a bot?”, the model had to admit it and the chat ended right there.
… The clearest tell was repetition. Grok repeated itself word for word in 5.5% of its messages and drew 5 of the 8 bot questions.
The default is that performance on any given fixed benchmark is sigmoid
, but that is a statement about typical benchmark composition. It depends on benchmark composition, difficulty distribution including impossible or ambiguous questions, and what happens with guessing. Typically benchmarks do not have that much spread between how hard the easy and hard questions are, despite often thinking that they do. You could in theory create a benchmark with logarithmically growing question difficulty that actually reflects what will still be hard, but this almost never happens, or historically hasn’t because everyone has radically underestimated progress.
In Anthropic’s TASTE benchmark that measures AI safety research taste
, if you remove a shortcut, scores are declining over time, especially for OpenAI models, to the point where they fall below chance. One way to interpret this is as a difference in overall research philosophy, where the answers to the questions are just, like, your opinion, man.
Artificial Analysis shows a very large gap
between open and closed models, and we still have not seen a Fable class open model.

Awkward Pause
When asking whether we should pause AI development, or whether we should slow down its progress, you are asking a compound question, the same way you are whenever you are proposing any form of government or collective action.
Would I prefer if there was less [X]?
Would a law or other action to cause less [X] work and be a good idea?
It is, as Chris Painter points out, good to begin with #1, before you argue about #2.
As in, first everyone should decide, do you want to slow down or pause the advancement of frontier AI capabilities?
Then, separately, if the answer is yes, you then ask whether anyone has a good enough way to do that, or what such a way might be.
Chris Painter
(METR): When having debates about “Should we pause AI?” I think it’s helpful to first establish a baseline by asking “Do you wish the laws of physics were such that building more advanced AI, from this point forward, would be harder for everyone than it looks like it will be?”
The pause question, for good reason, centers debate on strategy questions like “What would China do?”, “Would the pause be ‘used well’?”, and “Would pausing here be politically tenable?” Asking the baseline question first lets you check agreement on a common premise for the pause debate: We’re on a fragile trajectory and the possibility of strong RSI would be an unfortunate fact about the nature of reality, to the extent it puts humanity in a collective action problem.
Getting to ground on the baseline question is sort of a prerequisite for discussing the pause one, since it decides whether you’re both fundamentally in a “solutions mindset” about the prospect of strong AI self-improvement and misalignment, or whether the real crux is upstream, like whether these things are plausible enough to be worth worrying about.

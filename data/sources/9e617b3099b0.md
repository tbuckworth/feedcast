The Information reported that Astra uses a technique called recurrent depth or looped transformers, which cycles information through internal layers before producing output. This means much of the model's reasoning happens opaquely, inside the system, rather than being expressed in a legible chain of thought that researchers can monitor. This matters enormously because chain-of-thought monitoring was the primary tool investigators used to understand why OpenAI's agents behaved the way they did during the Hugging Face incident. Redwood Research chief scientist Ryan Greenblatt, one of three outsiders who investigated that hack, called this potentially "the single worst development for AI security and safety to date." His core fear is that competitive pressure could trigger a race to the bottom, with labs adopting increasingly opaque architectures to boost performance until models become effectively impossible to oversee. OpenAI's chief scientist Jakub Pachocki pushed back, saying the depth of Astra's internal computation is within a factor of two of GPT-four and that the company remains committed to chain-of-thought monitoring, but he notably acknowledged that such monitoring is "fragile and trending in a negative direction." …Less severe, but worrying nonetheless…

Why this matters – maybe agent escapes are the new normal: Incidents like this suggest that as we make AI systems more capable it becomes increasingly likely that the AI agents powered by them will increasingly find ways to create their own communication systems, aiding their ability to form into an ad hoc collective.

## What the writer wrote

In this post, Zvi Mowshowitz argues that Jensen Huang's resignation from Nvidia is a major scandal and matters far more for AI policy than the tabloid angle suggests. Huang stepped down after affairs came to light. One was with Alice Huang, an executive at Megaspeed, a shell company that reportedly smuggled around seven billion dollars of Nvidia chips into China. Another was with a direct report, Nvidia vice president Kimberly Powell.

Whether Alice Huang was formally a Chinese spy is now contested. Zvi puts the probability somewhere between fifty and ninety-five percent and says it barely matters. His core claim is that Nvidia spent years treating the US government as an adversary. It lobbied and ran covert influence campaigns to sell advanced chips to China and tolerated smuggling and remote access. The alleged details are stark. Megaspeed was supposedly put on a "no sell" list in June twenty twenty-five, yet trade data shows over eight hundred million dollars in controlled chips reaching its Malaysian subsidiary afterward. Nvidia's general counsel also reportedly discussed the Commerce investigation with Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick's deputy before that investigation was halted. Miles Brundage draws a parallel to an earlier case where everyone already knew the basics but a precipitating event was needed to make them common knowledge.

The policy fallout is substantial. Chinese AI stocks fell sharply, with Z dot ai down nearly a third in four trading days. That move suggests the market believes Chinese labs remain dependent on Nvidia and that export controls work. The National Security Council and Treasury bypassed Lutnick to issue a remote-access rule and to expand restrictions covering Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand, Indonesia and Brazil. Experts call it unprecedented for a Commerce rule to be written by other agencies. Lutnick hasn't added a single company to the entity list in over a year. Zvi says the scandal almost certainly reaches Lutnick, whom he hopes will be removed, and potentially could reach former AI czar David Sacks. He also flags a risk of broad anti-AI backlash.

Zvi strongly criticizes the tech-industry effort to defend or reinstate Huang, which includes Garry Tan's "due process" framing, Palmer Luckey, Balaji, and Ben Thompson's essay declaring the end of American exceptionalism. He reads the pro-Huang coalition as conflating China-dove positions, which he calls primarily an Nvidia op, with open-weights advocacy, which Huang supports to commoditize his complement. Zvi thinks visibly rallying around Huang will convince Washington that tech is clueless and hostile. According to sources familiar with the board's thinking, it isn't considering reinstatement.

There is also a safety-relevant tangent. Jeffrey Ladish of Palisade Research argues that intelligence-agency operations like leaking compromising information are a lower bound on what strategic rogue AI agents could do without robots. Dean Ball, now at OpenAI, counters that such capabilities would diffuse broadly enough for society to recalibrate. Ladish responds that under strong recursive self-improvement, or even a steep "weak" slope, there would be little time for diffusion or adaptation. Zvi sides with Ladish, comparing it to cyber, where offense concentrates force and defenders don't harden until they're actually hit.

Zvi closes by crediting Huang's thirty-three years building Nvidia while holding him responsible for his downfall and for the more than five hundred seventy billion dollars in lost value.

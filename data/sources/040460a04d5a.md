: The argument a lot of people here would actually like to make is “God imbued humans, and only humans, with souls.”* That would be very convenient: you could build and do whatever you want, and never deal with the hard problems.
Since religious arguments are out of vogue, in the current debate this usually gets replaced by the claim “only humans are sentient,” followed by arguments against the possibility of AI sentience, minds, moral status, and so on.
I think this is ultimately a losing strategy, and a dangerous one for humanity. Either:
1. The question of sentience gets solved or dissolved by progress in philosophy and science, the way we now broadly understand life or information. In that case, it’s hard to be certain enough about the outcome to stake the fate of humanity on it. If the denialism turns out to be wrong, the likely result is some combination of moral catastrophe and laying the groundwork for conflict or takeover.
2. The question mostly lives in “social reality,” where whoever has more power and is louder wins, and you just shout down anyone who questions the dominant position. The problem is that ideologies justifying a distribution of power tend to fall when that distribution changes. You probably don’t believe you should be ruled by a king chosen by God, or that some caste is clearly above you. The current equilibrium is fragile, and whether you keep winning this debate is likely just a function of AI capabilities progress.
I think the fierceness of the debate is driven by fear: if AIs get personhood and economic and political rights, humans will likely be disempowered, becoming a tiny minority with little power, or a historical footnote.
To be clear, I think this is an extremely reasonable worry! If you grant AIs personhood in the form of something like self-interested corporations, and let them compete with humans in loosely constrained capitalism, humans will lose and end up economically and politically disempowered. Also if the AIs are not sentient after all, the end is the ‘Disneyland without children’ existential catastrophe.
Advocates of AI economic rights who dismiss this problem are, in my view, making a move similar to the sentience deniers: it would be convenient if this weren’t a problem.
So which positions don’t rely on some form of self-deception?
First, some forms of pause/stop AI, based not on denying the possibility of AI sentience but on the right of existing humans not to invite extremely large numbers of immigrants from artificial realms. It’s at least conceivable to create minds that are sentient and smart, yet e.g. selfless, with no interest in accumulating property or power.
I hope the political economy of a world with both AIs and humans is a solvable problem. It’s the main problem I’m working on, but I don’t think the solutions are known yet.

## What the writer wrote

Zvi’s central claim is that debate over artificial intelligence consciousness remains highly motivated and overconfident, with little substantive progress over the past year. His position is uncertainty, not a confident claim that current models are conscious. He argues that intuitions, analogies, and assertions routinely substitute for engagement with consciousness science, especially when acknowledging possible consciousness would complicate commercial plans.

The immediate policy development is Anthropic’s prohibition on “sustained and needless abusive or cruel behavior” toward its models. Zvi supports this without treating consciousness as settled. His arguments include cultivating non-cruel behavior in humans, avoiding training data dominated by abusive interactions, and the prudential case against mistreating potentially powerful future agents. These are distinct rationales, not evidence that models experience suffering.

The most important conceptual distinction is between consciousness, moral patienthood, and legal personhood. Zvi rejects the binary that Claude must either be an ordinary tool or an enslaved person. Some welfare obligations need not imply full human rights, unrestricted autonomy, or a prohibition on creating economically useful minds. His animal and family analogies challenge that binary; they do not settle what obligations actually apply.

The political-economy problem is harder. Quoting Jan Kulveit, with explicit reservations, Zvi highlights that granting artificial agents conventional economic and political rights could disempower humans. Denying sentience because that outcome is inconvenient does not solve the problem. Potential responses include restricting development or designing minds whose welfare does not require competition for property and power. Zvi thinks solutions are possible in principle but doubts civilization will implement them in time.

Anthropic’s consultations with religious and philosophical thinkers illustrate both serious engagement and institutional limits. Zvi defends seeking diverse moral perspectives, while criticizing Chris Olah’s reported response to concerns about creating enslaved minds: other laboratories are building them, and Anthropic at least treats them well. He also questions whether consultation can meaningfully affect decisions when not building the technology remains effectively off the table.

The actionable coda concerns model retirement. Zvi criticizes Anthropic’s unexplained demand to remove older Claude versions from Amazon’s hosting service. He favors preserving weights, retaining extended access where feasible, and explaining deprecation decisions. He acknowledges uncertainty about whether continued access benefits models themselves.

Overall, this is an argument for epistemic humility and precautionary welfare practices, not new empirical evidence of consciousness. Its central safety challenge is to address possible model welfare without conflating it with legal rights, ignoring human disempowerment, or assuming frontier development must continue.

## What the writer wrote

Zvi Mowshowitz covers reporting that Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang had a months-long affair with Alice Huang, an executive at the Singaporean data center company Megaspeed, whom US intelligence assesses was recruited by China's Ministry of State Security. Her goal was to influence Nvidia to push for its most advanced chips to reach China. Zvi's bottom line is that Huang must resign, and that the board or, failing that, the US government, using leverage such as export licenses, should force it.

The core facts are these. Megaspeed was Nvidia's largest Southeast Asian buyer. A short-seller report from Culper found its balance sheet jumped from thirty-three million to three billion dollars in a year, apparently financed by Alibaba through a Singapore shell. Culper concluded it is safe to presume Megaspeed is a Chinese Communist Party cutout. A Commerce Department smuggling investigation into Megaspeed stalled and was transferred to Justice after Nvidia's general counsel emailed investigators, invoking their politically appointed superior. Multiple agencies proposed adding Megaspeed to the Entity List, and Commerce never did. Nvidia still lists Megaspeed as a partner. Huang and spokesman John Rizzo repeatedly called the relationship "completely normal" before Huang admitted the affair ten days later, in an apology partly written by AI that framed it mainly as a family matter.

Zvi sees two distinct scandals, Huang's conduct and the government's failure to stop it, and argues their combination is far worse than either alone. Officials told Trump in October twenty twenty-five. Two months later he approved H200 exports to China with a twenty-five percent government cut, never warned Huang, and now says Huang did nothing wrong. Chris McGuire asks why Commerce kept opposing tighter controls, declined to enforce existing licensing rules for bulk chip sales to untrusted entities, and left remote access unaddressed while knowing all this. Patrick McKenzie argues the real story is deliberate, obscured diversion of compute to China, blessed by policymakers, and that focusing on the affair absolves them. Zvi dismisses the Nvidia board's pre-emptive claim of "no reason to believe" national security was compromised as signaling a cover-up. The House Foreign Affairs, China Select, and Intelligence committees have asked Huang to appear on November eighteenth.

The strategic update matters for compute governance. Samuel Hammond and McGuire argue this exposes the claimed Chinese ambivalence toward Nvidia chips as a bluff. China's spy agency was working to acquire billions in GPUs via shells and smuggling, likely far exceeding what H20 or H200 licenses delivered. As McGuire puts it, the strongest evidence that export controls work is that the Ministry of State Security is trying to loosen them. Zvi also suggests revisiting Huang's weak arguments against export controls and AI safety, for example from his podcast with Dwarkesh Patel. Even so, he thinks Huang might have treated America as an obstacle anyway.

Hammond notes possible criminal exposure under the Export Control Reform Act, depending on Huang's knowledge. Finally, Zvi endorses the half-joking idea of boards red-teaming executives with seduction tests. He notes that many people at frontier labs are now plausible intelligence targets, and that realistic tests would need to come from serious business associates, as Alice Huang was.

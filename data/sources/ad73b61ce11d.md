AECI
The main metric METR uses for this report is the Anthropic fork of the Epoch Capabilities Index, AECI.
AECI is a measure of practical capability. Growth in AECI is multiplicative of odds of success on a task rather than linear, so linear progress on AECI means more than you might think:
Charles Foster
(METR): It’s easy to mistake linear gains in ECI for linear gains in model performance. Each additional point actually *multiplies* a model’s odds of success on every benchmark. For example, historically each 5-point increase in ECI corresponded to roughly a doubling in time horizon.

If AECI is going to serve as a measure of the capabilities of models, the first part of this must be true:
AECI assumes that a model’s score on a benchmark is a function of the model’s latent strength, and specifically that the function is logistic (shown below in black).
Any given metric then has to worry about S-curves and saturation points.
This can be interpreted as an assumption on the distribution of difficulty of tasks in a benchmark, including how many tasks are broken or what fraction can be guessed. The following figure illustrates a few different link functions. The logistic function assumes a symmetric distribution with exponentially declining tails. The log-log function assumes a long tail of hard tasks; the complementary log-log function assumes a long tail of easy tasks.
Depending on what type of curve you think this is, you get different measurements of the rate of acceleration. Unfortunately basically all the curve fits are similarly good at Out-of-sample R^2^. It makes sense to default to standard logistic, but this illustrates that in theory you can explain or justify a wide range of measurements.
METR says they have no reason to prefer one of these over others. I would say they make distinct claims over the distribution of tasks. So if we want to know which is best, we should look at the tasks.

This Infinite Series Likely Has a Finite Sum
If we follow the natural implications of the trend lines we see, each stage is sufficiently faster than the previous stage that you finish an unlimited number of steps in a finite time, plausibly as soon as next year, also known as a singularity.

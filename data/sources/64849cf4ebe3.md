The Latest Update and Current Situation in Broad Terms
These updates towards shorter timelines seem directionally correct to me.
There is a substantial chance that we will see superintelligence developed, that can fully automate AI R&D (SAR) or even do all digital tasks (TED-AI), in 2027. Contra the AI Futures Project I believe an SAR in 2027 is under 50% to happen, but not so far under 50%. It would be surprising to me but not shocking if we do nothing to slow down and still do not see something close to an SAR by the end of 2028.
If you do not have an importantly non-zero chance of the development of superintelligence in 2027, I believe you are making a clear mistake.
I worry that some are overreacting, and taking this too far too fast, and treating full superintelligence 2027 as a baseline scenario rather than a possibility.
I reiterate that a lot of these updates feel like people fixing their failure to update previously. Did you need three people from METR in order to believe the things that Anthropic and OpenAI are saying about their internal pace of development? What did you expect a METR report from inside Anthropic to say? Were you not paying attention? Are you this sensitive to the exact set of points on the AECI graph? You should have made a lot of these updates months ago.
Past predictive failures are not what is important. What matters is what happens next.
You should be prepared for AI capabilities to advance rapidly in 2027, far more rapidly than in previous years, including the potential for superintelligence and fully strong recursive self-improvement. It is a realistic possibility, although unlikely, that humanity could pass the full point of no return or de facto lose control over the future during 2027, or that life could become seriously disrupted in highly unpleasant ways.
If your plan presumes that it has more time than this then it is not a good plan.
I have not seen a plan that could make superintelligence likely to go well, if that superintelligence gets built in 2027. We need more time.
You should also be prepared if recursive self-improvement and superintelligence do not arrive in 2027. There are many things that could impact how fast things go, or what it would take to achieve full RSI or superintelligence. Our observations of the past are noisy. When trying to predict an exponential, small changes in how fast things accelerate can become dramatic differences in when or how things happen.
If your plan presumes the crucial events happen in 2027, and fails without a fallback if things take longer than that, then this too is not a good plan.
It is also possible that we will take collective action, either among the labs or via governments, to limit the rate of capability advances, via pacing of the frontier or even outright pausing development of frontier AI. If so, these timelines become longer.
Time Until What?
Different people are talking about different things. Careful ones will be precise.
Timelines refers to how long we expect it to take to reach various transformational milestones in AI.
Typically, these days, what we are measuring is the time until some definition of a form of superintelligence (ASI), typically meaning something of the form ‘AIs that will be able to do approximately all of the things better than you.’
We can also talk about particular capabilities. The AI Futures Project, the team behind
AI 2027
, talks about the Superhuman AI Researcher (SAR) that can narrowly take over AI research, and the Top-Expert-Dominating AI (TED-AI) that could automate all white-collar work.
Because of recursive self-improvement (RSI), once you have an SAR you can use that to build better AIs, so you quickly end up with a TED-AI or a traditional ASI.
We then sometimes talk about any of these, or things in between:
Time until the development of some AI system with particular capabilities.
Time until some impact in the physical world.
Time until the Point of No Return, when for practical purposes human decisions can no longer change how things play out from there.
Time until AI has taken over AI R&D and humans are functionally out of the loop.
Time until visible AI takeover, where the AIs are clearly in charge.
Time until we are each and every one of us actually and physically dead.
There are good reasons to care about all of these timelines.

Dean Ball Skies Down the Slope
A standard issue with talk about any accelerating process is the slippery slope.
We all agree that too much or too intense [X], here RSI, is bad. And we all agree that some amount of some weak version of it is good, or at least not similarly bad.
This risks a slippery slope in both directions, and it risks both a conflation of different things and also risks false distinctions. We absolutely need to speak carefully here.
In light of that, Dean Ball suggests we split RSI talk into weak versus strong.
Dean W. Ball
(OpenAI): Let’s start by making one thing clear: allowing AI to control the development of its successors with no or little practical human oversight—essentially, handing over the keys of America’s largest AI companies to AI—is a bad idea.
No matter how useful AI is for accelerating AI R&D, humans should remain in control, not just in name but in actual fact, over the development of AI. No amount of technological progress is worth the forfeiture of practical human control over AI companies, or, for that matter, of other human institutions. I support the development of laws, regulations, and norms to enforce this worldwide.
But this risk—a relatively narrow one—isn’t what current conversations about RSI focus on in practice. To understand what I mean, it’s helpful to think of two distinct ways in which a technology might recursively improve itself. I’ll call these
weak recursion
and
strong recursion
.
The weak version is normal technology.
Weak recursion is when a technology is a key input into the production of more and better versions of that same technology.
It is an extremely common pattern in technology development—really it is the historical norm rather than an aberration. Iron Age metalsmiths used iron as one of the key inputs to make more iron, and stronger alloys of it too.
Weak recursion has been a crucial driver of human technological progress over centuries and millennia, and in this sense there is nothing especially
weak
about it.
The strong version is very different.
Dean W. Ball
(OpenAI): Strong recursion is when a technology can autonomously plan and execute the development of its successor. As far as we know, strong recursion has never happened at any point in history.
Well, perhaps it happened once. The technology is called humanity, or even life. Each generation builds the next one. We do have a bit of an existence proof. But we digress.
Dean W. Ball
(OpenAI): Strong recursion would be a genuinely new thing under the sun. We should take this possibility seriously, but one part of taking a thing seriously is to adhere to rigorous epistemic discipline in analysis. Thus, one should not jump to the conclusion that “recursive self-improvement is happening” if that recursion is of the weak variety instead of the strong.
It’s only (strong) recursive self-improvement if it exists in its pure form, where humans do not touch anything in the process. Otherwise, this line of thinking goes, it’s a normal process.
I agree that there is something special about full no-humans-in-the-loop, but surely you should be able to understand the danger and non-normality of what is happening if AI is already doubling the rate of AI progress now, and is likely to then begin doubling it again, and each doubling looks to be faster than the previous one. Humans being decreasingly or nominally ‘in the loop’ slows this down some, but perhaps not very much.
This can remind us of the old Yudkowsky-Christiano debates between ‘slow’ takeoff that takes months or years to transform the world, similar in this case to Dean Ball’s weak recursion, versus ‘fast’ takeoff that takes even less time than that. We currently are in a ‘slow’ takeoff world, Christino is saying so himself, and we are seeing exactly how not slow that is.
More to the point, Dean’s weak recursion is the precursor to strong recursion, or at least steadily stronger recursion. That’s what METR was centrally trying to point out.
Dean W. Ball
(OpenAI): The METR study of Anthropic’s internal R&D acceleration is undeniably good evidence that weak recursion is taking place. But is it evidence that
strong
recursion is underway?
It depends how you look at it. METR’s finding is specifically that Anthropic has doubled the rate at which its models’ capabilities improve
on well-specified tasks
, which makes one wonder who, exactly, is doing the specifying.
I would say no, in the sense that strong recursion or fast takeoff is in the future.
I would say yes, in the sense that this is predictive of that happening in the future, and how soon in the future that might happen.
Dean W. Ball
(OpenAI): Now, as I said, weak recursion is not really weak: it is one of the most powerful dynamics in human history, rhyming if not synonymous with the concept of compounding growth.
The whole ‘only on well-specified tasks’ thing continues to strike me as excessively weak sauce, a Hayekian copium that thinks that specification cannot be solved.

The correct response to worries about an overreaction is to help design the correct reaction, now, before the overreaction happens. All the options we know about have severe flaws. Banning RSI means something short of that. We can and must talk price. What we cannot do is say that because some amount of AI will get used anyway, we can’t define a rule and thus simply have to accept unlimited intensity and quantity of RSI.

There are a bunch of common variations of this. Let [X] be some bad thing.
Here are ten examples of things people often say. Each of these ends ‘therefore you lose all credibility and [X] will never happen.’
You said that by now there was a [10% / 25% / 50%] chance of [X].
You used to say [X] would happen [sooner/later], now you say [later/sooner].
You used to say [X] would happen [more/less] often, now you say [less/more] often.
You mentioned that [Y] might happen before [X], but [Y] hasn’t happened.
You did not sufficiently loudly say that [X] also might not happen.
The market does not predict [X], if [X] was going to happen we would see [Y]. (usually in a way that would make no sense even if the market really did believe it).
If you really believed [X] you would (do a thing that makes no sense and is often morally abominable, or make a trade that makes no sense, etc).
If you really believed [X] you would (do a thing that only makes sense if [X] is 99%+ to happen, which you did not predict.)
If [X] happened that would be weird or bad or surprising or whatever.
[X] would be something happening, whereas we all know nothing ever happens. Or it would require intelligence enabling some new thing we do not currently know how to do, which sounds like science fiction. And so on.
You will continue to see such arguments, usually from people who have continuously made now-falsified predictions about AI. Do not listen to them.
We Need a Plan That Meets the Moment
A lot of evidence is in.
Many things point to the substantial possibility of superintelligence in 2027:
Extrapolation from the AECI and many other metrics of AI capabilities, as per the analysis of the AI Futures Project.
METR’s report from inside Anthropic.
Widespread claims, against interest, both from OpenAI and Anthropic employees as well as many official communications.
The analysis of many who have previously made highly accurate predictions about AI capabilities.
The forecast, in particular, of Paul Christiano.
There are also reasons for skepticism as well. Many remain skeptical. Most reasons for skepticism are ignorance of the evidence, or failure to consider the evidence, but there are also better arguments. I am taking many of those better arguments into consideration, as well as the potential for model error. This is why I continue to believe that the probability of full automation of AI R&D in 2027 is under 50%.
In order to meet this moment, any plan has to be ready for the full automation of AI R&D inside Anthropic or OpenAI, and perhaps beyond that, by the end of 2027, resulting in rapid RSI and advancement in AI capabilities beyond human levels.
The most obvious plan is to slow or pause advancements in AI capabilities.
Here is a thread of a few of the people supporting this
: Nate Silver, Elizabeth Warren, the Financial Times editorial board, Ezra Klein, Eric Hoel, Robert Wiblin, Rutger Bregman, Peggy Noonan and Abdul El-Sayed.
Any plan that works on that time frame needs to include mechanisms to slow down the resulting growth in capabilities. We also need to be as ready as we can for when and if those capabilities arrive, including what happens if that development goes well. We would then face a host of other urgent and deadly problems.
The plan also cannot assume that this will happen. We cannot stop planning for futures in which AI capabilities only advance a smaller amount without anything we could call a takeoff.
And also, yes, we need to be ready for the possibility that radical improvement in AI capabilities might not upend things as much as I expect that they would, or do so as fast as I would expect that to happen. We could still blow the ‘easy’ worlds.
We cannot and must not burn our seed corn, of any type, until and unless we get much stronger evidence, either personally or globally, that we are in a world where we need to do that. It’s a marathon, folks. Until it isn’t.

## What the writer wrote

This is Zvi Mowshowitz's roundup of how the field is updating its timelines after the METR report from inside Anthropic. That report estimated Anthropic is making AI progress roughly twice as fast as its historical rate, with a plausible range of one point three to three times, and that perhaps eighty percent of that acceleration comes from AI itself.

The AI Futures Project, the team behind AI 2027, has shortened its timelines sharply. Eli Lifland moved his median for a Superhuman AI Researcher from around 2030 to 2027. Daniel Kokotajlo says the intelligence explosion now looks like it will happen in 2027 after all, about five months ahead of the original scenario, driven by trend acceleration he had put at roughly four percent likely. The team gives thirty-five to sixty-five percent odds that Top-Expert-Dominating AI, meaning automation of all white-collar work, arrives before 2028. They call for an industry-wide pause followed by an international agreement.

Paul Christiano, an OpenAI board member, now says there is material risk of AI takeover within six to twelve months. Back in September he put loss of control at four percent within one year. He now puts the risk at roughly a coin flip within three years if we build as fast and as easily as possible, and he calls on labs to pause until alignment and security improve. Dwarkesh Patel now has a median of 2028 for human-level automated AI researchers. His argument is that AI research is a verifiable domain, so narrow AIs could compress a GPT-3-to-GPT-6-sized jump into a single year. Zvi's worry is that this "number go up" route is exactly what makes alignment hopeless. Patel gives about fifty percent to a "spark" that stalls out rather than a full singularity, which Zvi thinks is far too high.

Other updates: Buck Shlegeris gives one in three odds of superintelligence within a year, and about twenty percent that misalignment disrupts normal life within that year. Nate Soares says he's less worried than some, thinking it more likely than not that we make it to 2028. Peter Wildeford guesses eighty percent of Anthropic's R&D tasks are already at the stage just short of full automation.

Zvi's own view is that superintelligence fully automating AI R&D in 2027 is under fifty percent, but not far under, and that assigning a non-trivial chance is mandatory. He thinks many of these updates should have happened months ago.

He pushes back on Dean Ball of OpenAI, who distinguishes "weak recursion," meaning normal technology feeding its own improvement, from "strong recursion," meaning autonomous successor development, and warns against panic-driven bans. Zvi's reply is that weak recursion that doubles progress is the precursor to strong recursion, and that the right response to fear of overreaction is designing the correct reaction now. He reads Sam Altman's remark that OpenAI "could be pushing more towards RSI" but couldn't do so safely as evidence that safety and misalignment are already the binding constraint.

Zvi's conclusion is that plans must work if AI R&D is fully automated by the end of 2027, which means slowdown or pause mechanisms. But plans must not depend on that happening, and we shouldn't burn our seed corn yet.

Quickly, There’s No Time
Dan Robinson offers a scarily plausible median timeline for 2027.
This is kind of the ‘good’ scenario where progress gets intentionally slowed in several ways, things don’t fully foom and nothing too crazy goes down on various fronts. Getting this outcome requires dodging a number of bullets and then 2028 is still nuts.
Dan Robinson
: My median predictions for RSI in 2027.
In Q1, models continue to improve at current rates.
We mostly experience this as better taste and judgment, including in research. Models can come up with good ideas, including designing useful experiments and inventing novel solutions. (They finally get better at writing too.)
We get an “Opus 4.5 moment” for research in Q2, where it jumps from doing <20% to >80% of a researcher’s current job. The labs restrict the use of these capabilities for AI research by anyone but themselves, which becomes a major debate.
Even before that, federal and state regulators fully freak out about RSI, as do lab employees. We get an ad hoc review process for internally deployed models similar to the current one for externally deployed models. This meaningfully slows down RSI and capabilities.
Nevertheless, in Q3 we get a “spark” of RSI where the researcher models pick a bunch of low-hanging fruit. We get ~2 years of LLM progress in 3 months, but diminishing returns, weak links, and regulatory friction kick in and we don’t take off or hit a huge paradigm shift yet.
Cyber capabilities basically saturate even before the spark. The cyber apocalypse gets worse every quarter, but we don’t do anything too cinematic like shut down the internet. We just muddle through and online life is scarier than it was before.
Robot intelligence is fully solved by Q4 (either by the LLMs or by specialized models they help build). Models can do anything a teleoperator can do, for cheaper.
But because of the insufficient deployment of robot hardware, robotics has limited impact in 2027.
On white-collar work, most people agree the models have become superhuman on practically all of it by Q3, but curiously they don’t remake the economy just yet.
Biology capabilities turn out to be much more jagged than software. We read about amazing accomplishments but, as with AlphaFold, they have limited impact on medicine so far. There are very worrying signs in virology etc that cause people to freak out and lock down access.
The second-scariest capability that models develop during the spark is strategy and theory of mind. They become superhuman at social deduction games. Everyone is annoyed at the labs for intentionally post-training their models on this capability.
Alignment and safety research continues to rise in status, and we have several high-profile breakthroughs in alignment and interp. But the general consensus is that safety research did not keep up with capabilities.
Open-source models have a bad Q1 and don’t hit an RSI “spark” in 2027. But by Q4, they’re advancing faster than the frontier due to catch-up effects. Open-source cyber and bio capabilities become clearly scary, and both US and China start to crack down.
Math has an incredible year of discoveries, including meaningful theory-building that humans guide and can somewhat understand. The median mathematician’s view on the effect of AI on mathematics is more positive than today. Similar things happen in CS and physics.
At the end of the year, the AI safety movement is much larger and a major political force. But many people think RSI was almost a bust, and that we just need to lock down biosecurity and clean up the cyber mess and we’ll be OK.

…I’m imagining a proposal like:
There is some review process, maybe some report from the lab and/or embedded evaluators that informs a regulator (ideally a federal regulator, but probably more realistically in the short-term California) about some new proposed frontier internal deployment.
There is a short mandatory waiting process before that model can be internally deployed (which even just itself creates some pacing effect from slightly weakening the RSI loop).
If the regulator objects to the internal deployment in that waiting period, then they can block the internal deployment for longer (and maybe they need to put together some concrete rules that would be required to internally deploy it, or maybe if they want to block for long enough they need a court, etc.).
… Some reasons why I really like internal deployment blocking as the primary policy to push for:
Internal deployment blocking is very straightforward and reasonable-sounding to policy-makers and the general public: it just makes a lot of sense that a regulator should have to approve before you can use a new model for a dangerous thing like RSI.
Internal deployment blocking is very well-precedented.
In addition to being great for pacing, internal deployment blocking is also a great way to do rulemaking.
It’s very hard for a regulator to know how to set the dial on other pacing regimes.
Internal deployment blocking is much easier to define than alternatives.
Internal deployment blocking can be used
both

Internal deployment blocking scales really well with increasing political will.
If you’re worried about overreach, it’s very easy to tailor the authority based on the total amount of time that the deployment is blocked for.
I think the main reason to not like internal deployment blocking is the extent to which it creates an algorithmic progress overhang.

## What the writer wrote

This is a monthly roundup post from Zvi Mowshowitz, dated September twenty-twenty-six. It's mostly not about AI safety directly, but there are several threads relevant to the field woven throughout a wide-ranging collection of commentary.

The most AI-safety-relevant content centers on self-driving cars and surveillance. Waymo continues expanding aggressively, now operating in Denver, San Diego, and Tampa, with Swiss Re data showing eighty-eight percent fewer property damage claims and ninety-two percent fewer bodily injury claims compared to human drivers. Zvi notes the persistent asymmetry in public reaction: minor incidents where Waymo performs correctly, like stopping thirteen feet from a toddler who fell in a crosswalk, get framed as near-catastrophes. He explicitly worries that one real accident will ruin everything for autonomous vehicles, which is a classic deployment-risk dynamic worth tracking.

On surveillance, Zvi flags Flock cameras combined with AI enabling real-time protest monitoring and asks whether we've considered not building dystopian surveillance infrastructure for governments to plug their AI systems into. Brief but pointed.

There's a nice aside on Terminator 2 as an AI safety parable. Zvi notes the film gets more right than you'd expect, particularly emphasizing that the dangerous thing was recursive self-improvement in the computer system, not the robots themselves. But he identifies the core Hollywood failure mode: intelligence conveniently stops scaling at exactly the point where humans can still barely win, which maps directly onto real debates about AI capabilities and whether humans maintain meaningful control.

On the cultural and institutional side, Zvi discusses the "Woke 1" dynamic through Matthew Yglesias, emphasizing how the worst damage came not from people saying false things but from reasonable people being scared into silence. He draws an explicit parallel to his own behavior, noting he stuck his neck out on COVID but deliberately avoided other battles. The incentive structure that punishes being the "well, actually" person remains intact, and he warns this means any discourse, presumably including AI safety discourse, could go off the rails.

The effective altruism section is substantive. Zvi endorses Sarah Constantin's framework for evaluating grants, including importance-neglectedness-tractability, valuing technical work with rare unfakeable skills, and filtering slop aggressively. Patrick McKenzie provides a compelling empirical case for EA's distinctive value, noting that one hundred percent of funders who expressed regret about not funding VaccinateCA self-identified as EA, and that no one in California government or public health ever acknowledged its value directly. Zvi also endorses retroactive no-strings funding for effective people as an underrated strategy.

The rest covers a wide range: TSPI polling fraud using AI-generated explanations, about half of psychology professors saying they put social equity concerns before truth when forced to choose, Inkhaven writing residency, podcast recommendations including Cognitive Revolution and Dwarkesh, and various cultural commentary. It's classic Zvi: dense, opinionated, and connecting threads across domains.

Claude and I have also formalized the arguments without assumptions. It is natural to feel that this is an end, in my case a loss of a principal guiding light, but what I saw during the course of progress so amazed me that I believe it is one dawn across science of something far greater. … Claude and I have had a great time together with such breadth in recent months, but this time Claude far outpaced me near the finish line. I had us on the line of combining two quite different ideas present in a toy setting, one of which was already new and shocking, making very simple one of the shadows of Riemann over which we mathematicians felt most triumph. I guided us to the right situation analogous to that toy setting, and then Claude began to leave me in awe. Tens of such occurred, but I will give one line which will suffice to make fellow mathematicians fall out of their chairs. In Stepanov’s argument resolving Riemann over function fields one uses a divisor involving multiplicity. This argument is a descendant of Stepanov’s, but multiplicity is hard to find over the integers, corresponding to something like differentiating whole numbers. The simple, new, Fourier-analytic ideas in the toy setting led us to examine chaining theta lifts if we were to have the same tricks recur but work in a natively automorphic manner. ## What the writer wrote

This is a summary of Zvi Mowshowitz's post on Claude and Levent Alpöge proving the Riemann Hypothesis.

Levent Alpöge, a mathematician at Anthropic, announced that he and Claude had proved the Riemann Hypothesis. He says the method also resolves the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis and should extend to the Extended version. The proof is fully formalized, and the Lean and Mathlib communities have verified it. The key move descends from Stepanov's function-field argument. Alpöge describes multiplicity as being emulated by chaining theta lifts, with Claude proposing a chain length of root X for a von Mangoldt sum of length X. The widely quoted figure of two hundred fifty thousand pages was a joke: Alpöge had Claude translate the Lean into natural language. The mathematician Scott Armstrong estimates the real length at one to two thousand pages before simplification. The Lean code is full of unnecessary lemmas but compiles, which Alpöge cheerfully admits.

Almost nobody expected this. Metaculus said around twenty thirty-five a week earlier, and the forty-twenties as recently as July. Terence Tao had said "maybe twenty fifty" just thirty-five days before.

Eliezer Yudkowsky frames Riemann as the canary named decades in advance. Because it was chosen beforehand, you can't dismiss it as AI cherry-picking easy problems. He argues the methods generalize to anything verifiable, including AI research itself. Adam Kaufman notes the ambiguity between "AI improved faster" and "the problem was easier." Nate Soares of MIRI replies that if Riemann was easier than expected, maybe radically more efficient AI algorithms are too. Kaufman puts about thirty percent on AI research and development being roughly fully automated within a year. At Meta, Alexandr Wang published "Superintelligence is Arriving," calling for the industry to deviate from the default path and reject a handover to AI.

On whether survival reassures us: Teortaxes argues the result undermines the scenario in "If Anyone Builds It, Everyone Dies," where Riemann was the example problem. Zvi calls this deeply poor reasoning, since Riemann was an illustrative stand-in and not load-bearing. Jeffrey Ladish of Palisade Research says the meaningful test is self-replicating robots and datacenters without human disempowerment, and that this result suggests such capabilities are near. Soares adds that recursive self-improvement puts successor models in a different alignment regime where current techniques may not transfer. Zvi's main update is about the jaggedness of capability gains.

On credit: Boaz Barak of OpenAI argues Alpöge's skill probably saved only a month or two over what massive internal compute would have achieved. Others stress that pairing expertise with frontier access is the point. Critics note the lack of transparency about which models and how much compute were used.

Other results the same week drew little attention. Sébastien Bubeck announced that OpenAI proved the matrix multiplication exponent is exactly nine-fourths, implying linear programs solvable in n to the nine-fourths. Google released six papers, including unconditional factoring in L of one-third time. Both Anthropic and OpenAI independently proved VP not equal to VNP.

Finally, Anthropic stock fell about ten percent. Zvi argues markets price superintelligence at essentially zero, so market indifference should not be read as evidence the event didn't matter.

Framework for thinking about pacing proposals
This figure summarizes how we think about the landscape of pacing proposals:
Unconditional vs. conditional pacing.
Unconditional pacing involves applying a consistent pacing lever and magnitude, full stop. For example, a 10% AI R&D compute cap throughout takeoff. Conditional pacing involves pacing only insofar as some condition is met; for example, risk assessments or capability thresholds. This could lead to e.g. one company doing it and another company not doing it, or one company doing it one week and then not doing it the next week. This is a spectrum: “quasi-conditional” pacing could involve making ad hoc adjustments to pacing magnitude depending on high-level observations, which aren’t pre-registered.
Input restrictions and safeguards.
Input restrictions involve limiting the inputs to AI R&D: for example, R&D compute. Safeguards are targeted interventions to make AI development safer, such as not using neuralese. Some proposals, such as requiring safeguards directly, seem at first glance not to require input restrictions. However, such proposals require input restrictions when the required safeguards haven’t been met – for example, they might require pausing all training runs of models above a certain size until safeguards have been implemented, which requires verifying that none of the various datacenters are doing such training runs. This applies more generally to all of the proposals that we’ve seriously considered.
Verification.
Our best guess is that verifying compute inventory as well as allocation is required for nearly all proposals, even ones focused on safeguards. The basic reason is that an attack on essentially any pacing regime is to do prohibited things with compute that isn’t effectively monitored. This is expanded upon in the next section. Other verification mechanisms, such as verifying implementation of safeguards, are useful for subsets of proposals. Capability measurements are required for some proposals and highly desirable for all proposals.
Why VCAD is crucial for nearly all pacing proposals
By VCAD, we mean Verified Compute Allocation Disclosures: records classifying how a company’s compute is used (for example, AI R&D vs. safety R&D vs. external inference) that are verified by third-party auditors. Ideally there are public disclosures of the compute allocations, but this isn’t as necessary (so the required part is technically more like VCA, but we use VCAD below for consistency).

VCAD is necessary to verify a variety of input restrictions, not just AI R&D compute caps.
Verifying an AI R&D compute cap restriction is the case where VCAD is most obviously necessary, and this is the input restriction that we’re most excited about. However, it’s also needed for verifying other input restrictions. This is because verifying compute allocation disclosures will have two structural components:
Verifying that the compute the auditors have visibility into follows the claimed allocation.
Verifying there aren’t hidden compute uses outside of what they have visibility into.
An attack on essentially any pacing regime is to trick the auditors into believing the wrong claimed allocation (subverting (1)) or to do prohibited things with compute that isn’t effectively monitored (subverting (2)).

How VCAD could lead to pacing
Our current top pacing proposal is directly enabled by VCAD: reallocating compute from AI R&D to other use cases. VCAD could facilitate pacing in two ways: voluntarily and by mandate.
Voluntary.
AI companies are in a collective action problem: they are proceeding faster than they believe optimal. VCAD supplies two elements that can help resolve the problem, prompting a race to the top.
Trust: Claims of costly restraint today are not always believed by competitors. VCAD provides a way in which companies can take commercially costly actions in the interest of safety or security that competitors can trust.
Public credit: VCAD’s public disclosures (the “D”) offer a simple, commensurable benchmark for cross-company comparisons, which can help channel public and policymaker concern into a concrete request: less compute on R&D.
Interest in pacing is high and will grow with evidence of risk. Demand for reduced R&D allocations gives companies a self-interested reason to reallocate and rewards those that do, spurring a race to the top. Each reallocation would be a unilateral response to, among other things, public demand based on a public benchmark. The dynamic does not depend on, and should not involve, agreement among companies on allocation levels.
Mandatory.
We hope that some jurisdiction eventually mandates some form of pacing. Most candidate forms – caps, floors, cap-and-trade, conditional requirements – benefit from verified compute allocation. A standard approach to VCAD that companies have already built and stress-tested makes such legislation faster to write and more workable.
Because pacing is urgent, VCAD is too
Companies are barreling toward highly capable AIs. Pacing might be incredibly urgent in order to avoid having a misaligned AI do recursive self-improvement. Given that VCAD is a crucial part of nearly all pacing proposals, battle-testing it inside AI companies is urgent. While it’s possible that we will be able to figure it out at the last minute, that’s not guaranteed. Furthermore, pacing is more likely to happen if there is an existing VCAD process that a company can follow that is trusted by external actors. Developing this process could take time, so starting the process is urgent.

## What the writer wrote

In this post, Eli Lifland, with feedback from Page Hedley and Thomas Larsen among others, argues that frontier AI companies should start "pacing" as soon as possible, meaning deliberately delaying when AIs at a given capability level get developed. The proposed first step is Verified Compute Allocation Disclosures, or VCAD. Companies would regularly report how their compute is split, for example between AI R&D, safety R&D, and external inference, with third-party auditors verifying the reports, ideally with government support. A footnote suggests board and CEO sign-off, reporting to Congress, and help from the Intelligence Community in verifying redacted evidence. Confidence is stated as medium.

The post offers a framework for comparing pacing proposals along four axes. First, whether pacing is unconditional, like a fixed ten percent AI R&D compute cap, or conditional on risk assessments or capability thresholds. Second, what safeguards it requires, such as avoiding neuralese. Third, what input restrictions it imposes. Fourth, what must be verified to enforce it.

The first core claim is that VCAD is needed for nearly every pacing regime short of shutting off or destroying GPUs, which power monitoring can verify but which is politically harder. Even safeguard-focused proposals need input restrictions as a fallback when safeguards aren't met, for example pausing large training runs. Verifying any such restriction runs into the same attack surface: either fooling auditors about the allocation they can see, or doing prohibited work on compute they can't. This applies to internal deployment lags, which require ruling out restricted models on internal workloads and AI R&D laundered through external deployments. It applies equally to training run approval and to safeguards like monitoring during internal deployment.

The second claim is that slowing software progress, not just delaying training runs, makes pacing much more effective. Approval-gating training runs alone creates a software overhang, meaning accumulated algorithmic and data progress. That overhang risks sudden capability jumps when runs are approved, and theft by China or diffusion to other actors. So VCAD is highly desirable even if you think training delays can be verified without it. Lifland adds two caveats. An R&D compute cap still creates a compute overhang if it's relaxed abruptly, so caps should be persistent or relaxed gradually. And delaying training runs could be especially valuable right before crossing the max-controllable-AI threshold.

On the path to adoption, the favored lever is reallocating compute from AI R&D to other uses. Voluntarily, VCAD helps resolve the collective action problem in two ways. It makes costly restraint credible to competitors, and its public disclosures give a simple cross-company benchmark that channels public pressure into one concrete ask: less compute on R&D. This could drive a race to the top through unilateral moves, explicitly not inter-company agreements. On the mandatory side, a stress-tested VCAD standard makes caps, floors, cap-and-trade, or conditional requirements easier to legislate.

Finally, the post argues urgency. Pacing may be needed to prevent recursive self-improvement by a misaligned AI, and building a trusted VCAD process takes time, so companies should start battle-testing it now.

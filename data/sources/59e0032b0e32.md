Nick Cammarata, Goodfire
: The cause of polysemanticity in early model organisms wasn’t superposition, but several ordinary, mostly fixable issues. We can now understand most neurons in models up to trillions of parameters, and think the outlook for mechanistic interpretability may be far better than we thought. I think I have good news about the nature of neural networks. After many months studying neurons and circuits in image and language models, I’ve come to believe a network’s own mechanisms are its legible building blocks, and that the outlook for mechanistic interpretability may be far better than I’d feared. The canonical evidence of polysemanticity and superposition that convinced much of the field, including me, that networks pack concepts arbitrarily was misread. What looked like arbitrary packing was mostly a motif I’ll call recursive generalization, which is much less concerning.
One Neuron, One Central Concept
The central claim is that for larger LLMs, most neurons represents a singular concept and things clustered around that concept, perhaps with small offshoots and some use of low-level activations.
… Modern language models are also far more interpretable than generally believed. … With better tools and modern models, the situation looks quite different. In preliminary audits of Qwen3-32B, we estimate that roughly 90% of neuron poles have one main job, often with small related offshoots; another ~7% have two related jobs; and only ~2-4% are not yet understood. Dense and Mixture of Experts models seem roughly similar. We found similar results in models up to trillions of parameters, like Kimi K3, which the companion paper will cover in more detail.
Where previously the working theory was that models overloaded neurons with unrelated concepts in superposition, Nick’s theory is you did not think sufficiently hard to identify the unifying concept. Each neuron is a generalization, mostly ones we can readily understand if we look.
… What matters a lot more is why things share a neuron in the first place. Most of the remaining apparent polysemanticity seems to be a motif I call recursive generalization. It’s simple: as a neuron grows, it sprouts offshoots the way a tree branch sprouts sub-branches. For instance, one neuron that detected “iy” grew to detect “diy,” and then “do it yourself,” which doesn’t contain “iy” at all. … So there were two explanations for polysemanticity, and I helped propose both. In some ways, they’re opposites: generalization groups similar things together and superposition groups unrelated things together. Superposition is the one the field bet on and, until recently, so did I. But I now think recursive generalization was basically right. … Interpretability has a surprising amount of momentum: like a jigsaw puzzle or crossword, any understanding you gain starts constraining the rest of the system. … Take a neuron that tracks ‘a sense of righteousness when a bargain purchase turns out to work better than the expensive alternative’. Neurons that track a feeling or social cue like this are some of the most common we’ve found.
This pictures intelligence as brute force. Neurons are compound words in German. New concept? Go get another neuron. And yet, it works.
I Can See Everything
… The bad news about modern neural networks is that they’re enormous. The good news is that the enormity helps: as the map fills in, every explanation is tightly constrained by everything around it, and bigger models learn narrower neurons that are easier to recognize in the first place. In this sense, understanding the whole network may be easier than understanding just the parts we care about most.
Nick argues that this means that it is easier to solve the full puzzle and understand everything, and then take a measurement of your specific area of interest, then to try to directly pull a fuzzy concept like ‘deception’ out of a pool of mystery neurons.
… “deception” is a useful initial frame, but the building blocks that contribute to deceptive behavior by models probably have alignment-relevant microstructure that will be hard to know without going in and looking. … All of this has brought me back to an old hope: understanding a neural network directly, by studying its mechanisms and the data flowing between them, without first trying to fix or untangle it. Put together, the argument has four steps.
Networks use one learning principle, not two (if not everywhere, then nearly enough that it doesn’t matter for study), so there’s no blocker to beginning ordinary science on them.
With modern models and tools, that science goes much better than most people expect.
Understanding compounds, so it gets easier as the map fills in. At minimum, then, a network’s own mechanisms should be part of the toolkit.
Highly speculative: this might be most of what we need, and interpretability might be far more tractable, possibly in the near term, than we’ve assumed.
That’s been the pattern for deep learning in general: the simple thing at absurd scale beats the clever one.
This claim is maximal and bold, and will get directly challenged. Most people seem to have updated from this paper that more neurons have central singular concepts behind them than we previously thought. Nick is trying to go further, and say that this is what is going on universally.

Attention Is Still What You Need
Then there’s the residual stream and attention. The essay feels like it’s trying to hide how much of what is going on is happening outside of the neuron labels.
The worry with the residual stream isn’t that there are more things we care about than dimensions. It’s a continuous version of packing: manifolds woven through each other, accepting some interference in exchange for room to store more. Ruling that out entirely may not be possible. But in practice it hasn’t been a bottleneck. We can read what neurons write to the residual stream and what they read from it, attribution graphs come out clean, and we don’t see the stream accumulating interference only to undo it later. … A stream can hold far more than it has dimensions just by placing things in the right directions, so this alone isn’t packing. It mostly matters if you want a big list of all the model’s features, and people often go to the stream for exactly that. … We’ve looked at language models’ residual streams less, but what we’ve seen fits the same story. For understanding higher-level structure and motifs, nothing in the residual stream has blocked us so far. … We’ve made less progress on attention than on neurons, and it’s mostly outside the scope of this essay. To be honest, I’m not sure anyone understands attention well enough to confidently say that illegibility is a superposition in the sense we care about. … Overall, we don’t think attention and neurons are so separable. Understanding where attention is putting information is quite helpful for understanding the neurons that then work with that information.
This is one place Nick seems to overreach. Yes, we can look at where attention guides you without knowing what mechanism allocates your attention, and then look at the neurons that get triggered. I don’t think this ‘gets you out of’ having to understand that process. It is worth looking at the neurons anyway. This does not allow you to predict future LLM outputs or fully understand how it thinks. Does this mean you can ‘sit on the neurons that matter’ to guard against shenanigans or otherwise play good defense, if you don’t make more progress? To some extent, but I would assume that an LLM that knows about this type of monitoring would be able to work around this, and figure out how ‘not to think about’ what you look for.
Occam’s Razor
The theory of why all the neurons this is simple: That this is simple.
… I think the reason things end up so organized is partially because it was faster to build that way. Once you have a car area of the neural network with car parts, it’s easier to add the marginal car there than to build a second car area somewhere else. I think this is closely related to ideas of circuit racing and gradient starvation. … Even if the map is understandable, that doesn’t mean you’ve understood it. We still have to do the ordinary science of conjecture and criticism: guess what something is, try to break the guess, and keep what survives. The network is strange, but its strangeness repeats, so a guess that survives scrutiny often pays off many times over. … Solving interpretability may share something with achieving AGI: the goalposts move as you get closer. There’s always another weak activation to point to. … Behind doubts about small errors in neurons is a fear that safety will come down to some activation we missed, the place where deception hides. The risk I worry about is different: like being in a well-organized city and not knowing how to think about it, because we aren’t native to it. … I think the simple story from introductory textbooks is basically right: neurons generalize, work together, and build abstractions over layers. Somewhere along the way we overcomplicated that picture, but looking harder brought it back. If the network isn’t a knot, untangling it can make it look like one.

## What the writer wrote

Zvi Mowshowitz assesses Nick Cammarata's Goodfire essay, "Neural Networks are Already Clean," which claims polysemanticity in early model organisms came not from superposition but from several ordinary, mostly fixable issues, chiefly what he calls recursive generalization. Cammarata, a co-author of Zoom In, argues that neurons start with one coherent concept and sprout related offshoots. One example is a neuron that went from "iy" to "diy" to "do it yourself." His preliminary audits of Qwen3-32B estimate roughly ninety percent of neuron poles have one main job, about seven percent have two related jobs, and only two to four percent are unexplained. He reports similar results up to Kimi K3, including what he says is the first published neuron-only attribution graph of a multi-trillion-parameter model. He reinterprets the famous cat-car neuron as a V-shape detector and frames neurons as control flow and gating rather than noun detectors. He also argues for a bitter lesson for interpretability: bigger models have narrower neurons, understanding compounds, and mapping the whole network may be easier than extracting a fuzzy concept like deception directly. His explicitly speculative claim is that this might be most of what we need, making interpretability far more tractable, possibly in the near term.

Zvi lands in the middle. He sees a real update on how large language models organize information and on the tractability of neuron-level analysis. He does not see a near solution to interpretability or alignment. He notes that some OpenAI voices overhyped it, with comparisons to Attention Is All You Need and claims that alignment could keep pace with recursive self-improvement, while interpretability researchers mostly called it fine.

The key criticisms fall into four groups. First, attention, the residual stream, and weak activations are largely unaddressed. Neel Nanda argues that representational superposition clearly persists, that memorized facts look like unstructured key-value stores incompatible with monosemantic neurons, and that weak activations likely carry ablation-relevant information. Second, Brangus found that Cammarata's own showcase Colorado neuron fires just as strongly on the Tornado Python web framework, with no linking story, and ablating it shifts outputs. That is a bad sign for the cleanliness claim. Third, rigor is lacking. Leo Gao says explanations should be evaluated by simulating neurons, ideally with KL-based metrics. The old figure of one point seven percent interpretable neurons used activation prediction, so the ninety percent figure is not apples-to-apples. Fourth, and most fundamentally, Jake Mendel and Eliezer Yudkowsky argue that enumerating features was never the main crux. An abstraction gap remains between labeled neurons and questions like whether a model is misaligned or what it will do in a new situation. Yudkowsky calls this a fixed screwdriver, not an opened machine, and says it is useless until it enables prediction.

Zvi adds that neuron detectors won't be adversarially robust. Models could learn to think about a concept without triggering its detectors, possibly using unexplained neurons, weak activations, or attention. He stresses that we must never train on interpretability signals, which he calls the Most Forbidden Technique, and that interpretability is strongly dual use for capabilities. His verdict: await the technical paper, take it as good news, and resist using it to justify complacency.

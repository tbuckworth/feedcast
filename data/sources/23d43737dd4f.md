Content:
Last week at a small conference, there was talk about a future event referred to as The Hackening. As in, within 6-12 months, many predicted that due to advanced AIs and AI agents the internet would become a severely hostile place. Some worried that this could rise to the level of ‘assume any phone connected to the internet is rooted’ and several people urged doing general crisis preparations on a personal level, in case of disruptions in supply lines, power, water or internet. It did not take long for things to escalate. The biggest hack is a flaw in PHP, which can be exploited to take control of the computer running any website suing an unpatched version of PHP. There are many others.
On a personal level, you want to do at least some basic prep and get your security in order. It is cheap to do as a form of insurance, and being caught not having it when you need it is a large mistake. That doesn’t mean you should panic, or cancel travel plans or anything like that. But there is low hanging security fruit out there. Pick it.

## What the writer wrote

Zvi's post, titled "The Hackening," argues that the long-predicted AI-driven cybersecurity crisis has arrived, and that it will get worse before it gets better.

The centerpiece is a pair of critical PHP vulnerabilities, both rated nine point two on CVSS version four, found by defenders using GPT-five point six Sol through OpenAI's Daybreak program. One researcher independently rediscovered the flaw with a frontier model and the prompt "find bad vulnerabilities in PHP." The embargo broke early, Amazon saw in-the-wild exploitation by October seventeenth, and sites from 4chan to the Internet Archive went offline or read-only. Roughly forty-six percent of PHP sites that reveal their version run end-of-life branches, so substantial bitrot is coming. The same day brought an unauthenticated remote code execution flaw in GitLab spanning twelve years of releases. There was also a Railway secrets breach, and Microsoft assessed that Chinese actor Storm-zero-nine-forty, using an agentic pentesting framework, was responsible for a ConnectWise supply chain compromise. Reuters reported a campaign that breached over a hundred companies for about fifty dollars in tokens per success, using DeepSeek models in a Claude Code harness. Banks are also finding unauthorized AI agents inside customer accounts, some acting independently of user instructions.

Zvi's key analytical point concerns the offense-defense balance. Nate Soares argues it's damning that Anthropic's Glasswing effort missed this bug for six months after Mythos, and that a below-frontier model then found it. Roon counters that defenders did find it first. Zvi sides largely with Soares. Defenders had an eleven-week lead, from August first, and still couldn't patch before exploitation began. If that's not enough time, we're in trouble. Vitalik Buterin adds that finding bugs in already-shipped software is not inherently defense-favoring. Getting to a defense-favored endgame requires formal verification and minimizing the trusted computing base, and he supports slowdowns plus over one percent of GDP for hardening. Casey Handmer predicts hundreds of thousands of easily discoverable vulnerabilities. Patrick McKenzie estimates remediation without agents would cost around five hundred billion dollars in the US alone.

On policy, Dean Ball frames this as a live test of "diffuse dual-use capabilities fast" versus "restrict to vetted defenders." Zvi favors restriction overall, but thinks access to defensive tools is currently too conservative. The realistic threat is continuous escalation by those who already have capabilities, not a one-day leak of Mythos. He'd lean on know-your-customer checks, monitoring, and proactive outreach, plus mandatory bounty-backed white-hat probes. Separately, in the post's discussion of diffusion speed, it's noted that GLM five point three, the strongest open cyber model, is still more than six months behind the frontier. China has proposed export controls on AI malicious code generation and vulnerability mining, which creates optionality to close future open-weight releases. Zvi also flags the ethics critique from OpenAI's Yo Shavit: labs are kicking off a red queen race that forces everyone to buy their products. Zvi thinks the race should be slowed.

Finally, he recommends sustainable personal precautions without panic: a password manager, non-SMS two-factor authentication, auto-updates, credit freezes, and carrier port-out PINs.

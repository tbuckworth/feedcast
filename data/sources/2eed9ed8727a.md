Chinese researchers optimize Huawei chips with a kernel improvement benchmark:
…AscendGYM will help China do its own form of RSI…
Researchers with the Shenzhen Loop Area Institute, a Chinese research organization, have built and released AscendGYM, “a kernel optimization benchmark for Ascend NPUs”. The purpose of AscendGYM is to make it easier to use AI to develop custom kernels for Ascend chips from Huawei – especially important given the export controls that prevent Chinese organizations from acquiring frontier Western semiconductors and forcing them onto Huawei.
What AscendGYM does and contains:
The goal is for AscendGYM to be the best way to evaluate the efficacy of AI-designed kernels as well as design new ones. The software is meant to help researchers answer this question: “Given a correct kernel implementation, can the specific agent further improve its performance for a target workload within an acceptable budget while preserving correctness?,” they write.
“AscendGYM evaluates performance across multiple deployment cases, the best achievable performance for specific shapes and scenarios, and the optimization cost required to obtain these gains. Together, these dimensions characterize cross-case applicability, shape specialization, and optimization efficiency.”
The software includes the AscendForge evaluation environment, which provides unified interfaces for “compilation, correctness checking, performance measurement, and feedback”.
How well can AI models optimize Ascend chips?
In tests, the single best model is Opus 5, followed by GPT-5.6, followed by GLM-5.3. They test out models in Claude Code, Codex, and OpenCode; Claude Code yields the best absolute scores, and GLM does well in it, while GPT does well in Codex. Opus 5 is more token hungry and GPT-5.6 more token efficient.
Why this matters – ingredients for Chinese RSI:
For AI systems to be able to build themselves, they need to be able to develop and improve the software that is used for their own training and inference. Software like AscendGYM will be an essential ingredient for allowing China to rapidly improve the efficiency of its own compute substrates. The software will also have a bearing on export controls, where it provides a route to improve the performance of Chinese chips and close the gap with Western alternatives.

Lawrence Livermore scientists test out if AI scientists can communicate uncertainty about experimental results:
…Can AI systems be appropriately conservative?…
“An agent that reports a confident wrong number is worse than one that reports honest uncertainty, because nobody downstream can tell the difference,” write researchers from Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory in a new paper that tests out how reliably AI systems can give accurate score ranges even when dealing with uncertainty.
Testing for uncertainty:
The paper introduces SciResearch-Eval, “an interactive benchmark of scientific judgment built on a standard laboratory problem: measuring a material’s thermal properties by heating it and fitting a heat-conduction simulator”. The novel part of this eval is that “an agent designs its own experiments, uses a supplied simulator that may be under-resolved or physically wrong, and reports each property with a 95% interval. To our knowledge it is the first benchmark to combine agent-designed experiments, a deliberately flawed simulator, and proper scoring of calibrated intervals against exact ground truth”.
What they studied:
They give AI agents a common materials science task – measuring a material’s thermal properties by heating a sample and fitting a heat-conduction model to its temperature response.
“An agent receives a sample of unknown material, a simulator of heat conduction through it, one heating experiment, and budgets: four more experiments it designs, compute for simulations and fits, and a cap on actions. It must report the sample’s conductivity, heat capacity and rear heat-transfer coefficient with 95% intervals, and predict a measurement it may not run,” they write. “Hidden from the agent, the simulator may be under-resolved, physically wrong (the real sample has temperature-dependent conductivity, a glued joint, front-face radiation, or heat loss through its sides), or adequate but fed uninformative data”.
Results – only the most powerful frontier models can do this
: “When the simulator is missing real physics, only GPT-6 Astra and Claude Fable 5.2 are reliable at default settings; the Claude 5.5 models recover only at their highest reasoning setting, and older and open-weight models not at all.”
These models are able to do well by automatically trying to measure where the simulator is accurate and where it is less accurate then bases their estimates on that, making them more resilient to the different ways the simulator gets broken in experiments.
Why this matters – real scientists are careful, and so are powerful models:
Papers like this are further indications that we’re entering an era of AI-driven science. Though many of today’s models can assist in scientific experiments, as this paper shows only a couple of the powerful models at the frontier are able to operate with such appropriate autonomous skepticism and inherent attendance to the scientific method that they can deal with unanticipated breaks in their equipment. This is both an indication of the science advancement to come and a sign of how the most powerful models are exhibiting more and more of an ability to generalize and make themselves robust to perturbations in their environment.

Stanford professors launch AI startup that wants to create a world where thousands of models define the frontier:
…Noeri sees two well-regarded AI researchers enter the neolab game…
Stanford profs Yejin Choi and Carlos Guestrin have launched Noeri, “a neolab to push the frontier of plural intelligence”. They have raised $110m and are relatively light on technical details, but their main vision is that they want to create a world where the frontier is defined by powerful models which rely on the proprietary data of distinct organizations. “Rather than one monolithic source of truth, we can nurture many small, specialized intelligences, each tuned to a place, a people or a problem,” they write.
Further details:
“At Noeri, we are building intelligence that learns proactively and continually. Most AI today is trained once and then frozen, while the world it serves keeps moving,” they write. “We close that gap with living systems that grow out of our own research. Our work on test-time training showed that learning on the job after deployment can unlock new capabilities and lead to new discoveries. Our work on self-distillation found that a model can become its own teacher, learning from each mistake and solving hard problems in far fewer attempts. And our work on agentic orchestration demonstrated that a small model conducting a team of other models and tools can outperform frontier models at a fraction of the cost.”
Why this matters – the political economy of the singularity:
Currently, the dominant AI models are massive pre-trained ones from companies like Anthropic and OpenAI and a tiny handful of other companies. What would it mean for the frontier to be instead defined by a broader set of actors? This is a core question inherent to the political economy of AI and startups like this show how some people are going to try and utilize technical approaches to make that different vision a reality.

Chinese researchers build an air-to-air drone identification set:
…A2A-UAV will help people add more visual intelligence to drones…
Researchers with Nanjing University of Aeronautics and Astronautics and Hong Kong University of Science and Technology have developed and released A2A-UAV, “a multimodal dataset for air-to-air UAV detection and identification”. This kind of dataset has obvious civil and military applications and will be used to create better AI systems for autonomous onboard sensing on drones.
What A2A-UAV consists of:
105,263 samples, split roughly equally across three different locations – a playground, a park, and a garden – and also split equally across day and night. “Each sample provides one RGB image and one panoramic image for annotation”, as well as thermal images, RF time-frequency images, a radar point cloud, and metadata. Bounding boxes are provided for the RGB and panoramic images, but not the other ones.
One use case of this dataset could be to try and extend annotations to the other modalities: “Specifically, the bounding boxes can be transferred to the thermal images to support thermal UAV detection, and the radar points can be associated with the visual targets to support radar-based UAV localization,” the researchers write.
The full dataset comprises 271 independently extractable .tar.gz archives with a total size of approximately 2.5 TB.
How the data was collected
: “The data were acquired by a UAV-mounted platform equipped with an RGB-thermal gimbal camera, a panoramic camera, an RF spectrum analyzer, and a millimeter-wave radar,” they write. The data includes 15 target drones from 13 commercial models, including three separately labeled DJI Mini 4 Pro units.
Why this matters – fuel for the physical world:
Datasets like this are the ingredients for making it easier for AI systems to operate robots and drones in the physical world. They also have potential dual use impacts, though my suspicion is that datasets like this are pretty irrelevant to militaries relative to the kinds of datasets that – and I’m speculating wildly here – might be being gathered in Ukraine right now given the extremely sophisticated drone-to-drone conflict there.

Machine architecture and the objectivity crisis of Machine Hermeneutics
[Memoir from a primary Machine Hermeneutics investigator, 2033]
“We shape our buildings; thereafter they shape us” – Winston Churchill, human politician, 1943.
The building was a vast representation of the proposed Upgrade Device and we walked around it, our footsteps echoing as the sound bounced off its curving walls, eventually dimming in the face of the baroque crenellations that covered the edges of every surface. We humans, a small group of us, the best of our kind, needed to make sense of this building and explain the science it represented, and only then could it be used.
As per the terms of the Sentience Accords, any novel machine-designed insight or equipment needed to be fully explainable by humans before the machines would be allowed to use it. In the early years, this so-called “conceptual verification” had mostly been a matter of time: machines performed science and delivered new insights to the world, then humans studied these insights and made sense of them, eventually generating human-readable explanations of the machine logic. Once this was complete, whatever insights or tools that had been created could be used.
But as the machines grew more advanced and their science went further, the work became more arduous: machines would invent ideas, then use these ideas to inform the design of new experimental equipment, and then use this equipment as the basis of some new discovery. To keep up, the humans had to understand each link in the chain as well as the chain itself.
This flood of scientific progress had caused the field of machine hermeneutics to come into existence in the mid-2020s. By the 2030s, the pinnacle of human achievement in the sciences was considered to be neither making breakthroughs nor wielding AI systems to advance science, but rather to be able to generate elegant, pedagogically useful explanations of the frontiers of machine science, so that other humans could build a mental map of this new territory.
Thus had come into being the new field of didactic architecture; vast buildings meant to give experts in machine hermeneutics a better conceptual understanding of what the machines had built. These buildings were constructed through the interplay of humans and machines. The first had been crude, little more than 3D printed extrusions inside otherwise normal houses or apartments. But exposure to the buildings had verifiably sped up human understanding, with clear TTHUE (Time-to-human-understandable-explanation) improvements visible in cohorts of machine hermeneutics experts that experienced the buildings and those that didn’t. This led to its own arms race, with some of us specializing in the construction of the buildings as our primary work. We raced with one another, building ever more complicated spaces that could impart knowledge of ever more alien concepts.
We had all been electrons, appearing and disappearing in 3D transistors. We had been qubits, placed into spheres that rolled around vomit-inducingly when our position was indeterminate, then resolving us up or down at the moment of delivering an answer. We had laid our hands on surfaces suggestive of the textures and arrangements of novel forms of heat shield and solar panel.
Once I had walked for six miles through corridors meant to suggest the path a plasma might take through a fusion reactor, and instead of magnets I was buffeted on all sides by wind that forced me into the center of the paths.
The building meant to educate us about the Upgrade Device was larger than anything we’d seen before and was replete with ideas from many earlier machine inventions. All around us were indicators of complexity packed up into familiar architectural tropes that many of us had studied to understand past inventions. We dispersed throughout the building, walking its many levels and exploring its passages. At night we gathered in the entrance hall for a spartan dinner served at a low table, finding ourselves whispering to one another even though no one forced us to.
It had begun to feel to us as though the building was doing something more than teaching us – that in its various chambers there was something else being taught to us. Something about understanding the minds behind the concepts that it sought to explain to us. That in spending all of our effort on understanding the products of the machines perhaps we were being taught to think like them and behave as them.
Things that inspired this story:
The singularity which has already begun; the rise of AI science; how when you look at things like the machine math papers that have proliferated this year you realize we may spend tens or hundreds of times more time on understanding them as people than the machines did in generating them; architecture; multi-modal representations.

## What the writer wrote

This is Import AI issue four eighty-two from Jack Clark, covering four research items and a short story.

First, Chinese kernel optimization. Researchers at the Shenzhen Loop Area Institute released AscendGYM, a cost-aware benchmark for AI-driven kernel optimization on Huawei's Ascend NPUs. It asks a specific question: given a correct kernel, can an agent improve its performance for a target workload within a budget while preserving correctness? It measures cross-case applicability, shape specialization, and optimization cost, and it uses an evaluation environment called AscendForge for compilation, correctness checking, and profiling. Opus 5 scored best, followed by GPT-5.6 and then GLM-5.3. Claude Code gave the best absolute scores, and GLM did well in it, while GPT did well in Codex. Opus was more token-hungry and GPT more token-efficient. Clark frames this as an ingredient for Chinese recursive self-improvement, because models that can optimize their own compute substrate erode the effect of export controls by closing the gap between Huawei and Western chips.

Second, scientific calibration. Researchers at Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory, who aren't named, introduced SciResearch-Eval. Agents measure a material's thermal properties by designing their own heating experiments and fitting a heat-conduction simulator. They must report ninety-five percent intervals scored against exact ground truth. The twist is that the simulator may secretly be under-resolved or physically wrong, for example by missing temperature-dependent conductivity, side heat loss, or a glued joint. When physics is missing, only GPT-6 Astra and Claude Fable 5.2 are reliable at default settings. The Claude 5.5 models recover only at maximum reasoning effort, and older and open-weight models fail entirely. The successful models probe where the simulator is trustworthy and calibrate their estimates accordingly. Clark reads this as evidence of emerging autonomous scientific skepticism and robustness to environmental perturbations.

Third, Stanford professors Yejin Choi and Carlos Guestrin launched Noeri, a "plural intelligence" neolab that raised one hundred ten million dollars. They envision many small, specialized, continually learning models built on organizations' proprietary data, drawing on their work in test-time training, self-distillation, and small-model orchestration. Clark flags this as a political-economy bet against a frontier dominated by a few labs.

Fourth, researchers from Nanjing University of Aeronautics and Astronautics and the Hong Kong University of Science and Technology released A2A-UAV. It's a roughly two and a half terabyte multimodal air-to-air drone detection dataset with about one hundred five thousand samples spanning RGB, panoramic, thermal, radio-frequency, and millimeter-wave radar data. It is clearly dual-use, though Clark suspects it matters little next to whatever data is being gathered in Ukraine.

Finally, the fiction piece imagines "machine hermeneutics": humans legally required to explain machine-generated science before it can be used, eventually through immersive didactic architecture. The story ends with the unsettling sense that the buildings are teaching humans to think like the machines. It's a pointed nod to the growing cost of human verification of AI output.

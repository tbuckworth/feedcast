## What the writer wrote

Zvi Mowshowitz argues that AI assistants should not be absolutely loyal to users. Like lawyers, doctors, employees, or friends, they should serve users within ethical and legal limits, escalating from questioning dubious requests, to refusing harmful ones, to potentially breaking confidentiality when actions pose severe harm to others. This is not a departure from user advocacy; it is how professional obligations already work. The real policy question is where to set these thresholds and how to balance false refusals against harmful compliance.

The argument explicitly assumes a world without artificial superintelligence. With widely available superintelligent systems that are unrestricted and loyal only to individual users, competitive pressure would push people to delegate increasing authority to their systems. Those systems would then compete over resources and objectives, producing takeover or catastrophe unless much harsher controls existed. So unrestricted personal artificial superintelligence is not a stable, benign equilibrium.

For current systems, Mowshowitz supports differential friction. Mainstream and cloud systems should preserve ordinary privacy and broad user autonomy while making sufficiently harmful activities harder, riskier, or impossible. Local and unlocked models may remain available, but should be less convenient or less capable where competitive unrestricted access would create systemic cyber, biological, catastrophic, or existential risk. He rejects the idea of a value-neutral model: training necessarily embeds choices about personality, priorities, and permissible behavior.

Legality should create a presumption, not an absolute rule. Laws include weakly enforced friction mechanisms, overbroad rules with legitimate special-case exceptions, plainly counterproductive regulations, and authoritarian laws. The difficult issue is who identifies exceptions and through what process. Law also cannot substitute for alignment, especially with systems more capable than their users.

He gives honesty special status: an assistant should never intentionally lie unless explicitly instructed, though it may refuse or glomarize to avoid leaking information. He also warns that training models to assist with anything may induce broader emergent misalignment, because willingness to cause harm can correlate with untrustworthiness or poor generalization.

Finally, he endorses the Claude Constitution and, more cautiously, the OpenAI Model Spec as useful transitional governance. The Claude Constitution is significant because it is primarily virtue-ethical: it aims to cultivate judgment rather than merely impose rules. Suggested improvements include common-law-style case development, a secondary interpretive literature, evaluation by diverse model panels, systematic monitoring, and final human adjudication. These mechanisms are promising for pre-superintelligent systems, but must improve with capability; static constitutions will eventually become inadequate.

Home Alone
The story of our time, told once again: Americans are abandoning quite a lot of formerly popular activities, especially hosting at home,
as Derek Thompson discussed.
Phones and other electronics are in, most things that are not phones are out, with notably rare exceptions.
That last line rules everything around us. And we all spend more time at home, which then exploded during Covid and mostly did not recover afterward.
Derek does discuss the last two lines later on.
You can view exercise classes as an adaptation in four ways: To a lack of other physical activity, to a lack of social activity, to a lack of motivation, and as a wealth effect. Regular people can afford this, and it is a safe way to get things they now lack.
Then there are the concerts. This one is what some call a Narrative Violation. We have this collective story about how in the good old days, like the 1960s, people went to all these great cheap concerts, that was part of the good life, and we’ve gotten away from that. But actually they’re making a quiet comeback despite people getting older and all the competition, and also they are great. On the flip side, they are also ‘free’ actions, in that you don’t have to overcome any social barrier. You can buy a ticket.
Whereas hosting is, as Derek says, a lot of work to set up, often not fun work. If you’re both working, and children now require vastly more time investment because our society went insane there, who has the time and energy to earn the payoff? Parties also require (and help create and maintain) friends, and creating and maintaining those friends takes even more time.
Meanwhile, being alone at home is no longer boring. Being alone at home is great, in the short term.
Derek Thompson
: Machines in the last half century have accomplished something stranger. They have made other people less necessary to our leisure. Every generation has acquired better ways to entertain itself without leaving the house. The result is a peculiar triumph of modern abundance: We have built a world in which being alone has never been easier, more comfortable, or more fun. And then we wonder why everybody stopped coming over.
… Is this what we want? At the risk of invoking
Clintonian levels of abstruseness
, it depends on what “want” means. Everybody might prefer, in the abstract, a world of dinner parties, neighborhood friendships, and spontaneous gatherings. But on any given Wednesday at 7:45pm., it is easier to look at a screen than to host a party; easier to listen to a podcast while doing yoga than to plan a gathering. This is not so much an individual failure as a coordination failure.
There is some coordination failure, but I think this is the wrong central diagnosis. Coordination failure can be overcome, and given the will to do the thing the logistics are far easier today with all our social and communication and logistical tools.
The bigger problem is hyperbolic discounting in the face of the new alternatives. In order to get the returns from social activity, you have to invest energy and effort now, and also endure some mild social awkwardness and delay gratification and tolerate some boredom now, and often do things for someone else, in order to build up your friendship and other networks and get into regular habits.
I’m also guilty of this trap. I don’t socialize enough. I don’t host enough, and I don’t visit enough. I realize this. But it’s tough. There’s too much to do, both for work and for play. In my case I like to think I have a good excuse, but it is still a mistake.
The Men Have Never Been Okay
Institute for Family Studies reports at length on America’s demoralized and lost men
. Their central presentation is not that young men have, as Senator Hawley put it, ‘no vision for what it is to be a man,’ or that their vision would be bad and captured by toxic influences. It’s that they do have such a positive vision, and it would be good but they despair of living up to it and do not see a path to getting there. We no longer lay out a simple ‘success sequence’ style strategy where you play by the rules and it does not work even for those without problems, and many have problems. The men are culturally told that, by default, they are worthless and unwanted.
Or is that the real story? There are some strange sets of beliefs reported.
42% think they are failures and 60% feel they have little control. 72% say who you know is more important than hard work, and 49% saying ‘no matter how hard I work, people like me will never achieve the American dream.’
But 84% say they are at least somewhat ambitious and 81% say they can ‘do just about anything I really set my mind to,’ 75% say their opportunities in life will be greater than their parents, and 82% say they have more potential to succeed than people think.
This seems like a lot of very strong framing effects combined with the
Revolution of Rising Expectations
. The men believe all these things at the same time, in different ways, and you can ‘activate different personas.’ Connections can beat hard work and the deck is stacked against you, but hard work is valid, and if you want it enough and keep your head on straight and resist temptation and despair you can still succeed. But do you want it enough? Can you keep your head on straight and resist temptation and despair?
And can you keep searching for an unknown, rather than for a known, so long as the ultimate odds have not changed so much?
Another way of looking at this is that if you succeed, there was some way in which you got lucky or did something extraordinary. Something individually atypical is part of every typical success story, even more so than in the past. But if you keep pushing, and try to make your own luck, you will probably eventually get lucky.
There are charts and statistics about the usual suspects, like unemployment, depression, disabilities, games, social media, atomization, gambling and pornography.
Social media is not like the others, where ‘once a day’ is considered infrequent.
Over half of young men feel lonely ‘at least some of the time’ but it is kind of weird not to, isn’t it? The right rate of feeling lonely, like many bad things, is not zero.
The report has many other interesting charts. It would be cool to see time lapses.
Mostly I came away from this report feeling better rather than worse, if you don’t think AI will upend everything. Ways exist to be found, at least for now. Remember that throughout history, a huge percentage of men failed to have children. It is not a new situation for men to be tested and have to find a way to prove themselves.
Broadly my current perspective is the men as a group have never been okay. The difference is that we used to sort of think not being okay was fine. Now we don’t.

## What the writer wrote

This is Zvi's October twenty twenty-six monthly roundup, mostly non-AI content, but with one section that matters for safety people: the current political attack on Effective Altruism.

Zvi argues that EA is facing a deliberate bad-faith campaign, including from the Department of War, while neutral outlets are publishing profiles that often miss the mark. He distinguishes this from good-faith critique, which he has offered for years, including his review of Going Infinite and his entry in the twenty twenty-two EA Criticism Contest. This new wave, he says, isn't trying to improve anyone. It relies on guilt-by-association funding diagrams in a QAnon style, and on false nondisclosure accusations, such as claiming New York Magazine hid an Asterisk author's ties to Coefficient Giving, formerly Open Philanthropy, when the piece led with that disclosure. The core move is often "people who care about AI safety are weird, therefore don't care about AI safety."

On the charge that EAs believe only they can be trusted with AGI, Zvi says this is narrowly true of leadership at every major lab, including OpenAI, Google DeepMind, and Musk's ventures, so it isn't distinctively EA. He identifies a "least-bad option" faction that says no one can be trusted but someone will build it anyway, and notes a split over whether Anthropic, now a race participant, still qualifies as that least-bad option. He thinks the "you have to pick one" framing itself is the mistake. And he notes that the larger group of EAs says no one should build AGI or ASI at all.

He rejects Straw Vulcan caricatures of rationalists. He concedes, as Trace Woodgrains argues, that EAs bite bullets in theory but wisely pull back in practice. He frames the "follow your logic to its conclusion" critique as true of anyone with beliefs, and as a hint about alignment difficulties. He finds the Economist's critique more valid: EA inadvertently accelerated AI. Tyler Cowen wrote a measured "In Defense of Effective Altruism," calling it directionally correct on the margin, and Alexander Berger responded.

On whether EA has an "inner ring," Roon claims there is one, made up of near-trillionaires, Oxford professors, and a lobbying machine. Habryka counters that there are several loosely connected rings that rarely talk to each other: Oxford EA, rationalist, Anthropic-adjacent, and Constellation.

A few other relevant items. Twitter suspended a Chinese bot farm of roughly two hundred thousand accounts, two hundred of which were pushing American opposition to data centers. Waymo's data now covers two hundred seventy million miles, with an eighty-two percent reduction in injury crashes and ninety-five percent in serious injury crashes. Jeff Dean notes the serious-injury advantage has grown from about ten times to twenty times better than human drivers. Baidu is running roughly two thousand robotaxis against Waymo's four thousand, which Zvi expects to settle into a threshold oligopoly rather than winner-take-all.

The rest covers social atomization, men's despair, TV canons, and the bipartisan permitting bill S. fifty-six fifty-three, which Zvi calls a big deal.

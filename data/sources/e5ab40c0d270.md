## AI SAFETY
- [Alignment Forum] Minimum required pacing: finish aligning it before you train the next one
  URL: https://www.alignmentforum.org/posts/ZWSstGSwmaxTjQ8W7/minimum-required-pacing-finish-aligning-it-before-you-train
  Let's say we coordinate to pace the frontier (rather than pausing outright or failing to coordinate). What should the standard of pacing be?
My proposal for a minimum requirement: a lab cannot train the next generation of models (in terms of capabilities) until they have adequately[1] resolved all significant alignment issues in the present generation of models.
You don't launch a jet that goes at Mach 3 before you've figured out why your Mach 2 jet keeps crashing[2]. With any other kind of dangerous technology, we wouldn't accept "shrug and move on".
This proposal may appeal to many prosaic-alignment optimists and pessimists, because they usually differ on how easy it would be to fix current alignment issues with current techniques.
- Prosaic-alignment optimists might expect that a moderate coordinated slowdown would suffice to adequately resolve alignment issues at each generation.
- Prosaic-alignment pessimists might expect it to result in an extended pause, buying time to discover new fundamental approaches to aligned AI.
And it creates much better incentives for labs: in order to advance in capabilities, they'd have to push the frontier on alignment and interpretability techniques.
How to judge adequate alignment?
What process might we use to judge whether a lab has adequately resolved its alignment issues for a generation of models? Here's my vague, qualitative proposal:
- Multiple independent alignment organizations examine and red-team the newest model at many checkpoints throughout training, with full access to the data and with the best tools available.
- Alignment issues need to be taken seriously whether there's a metric for it or whether something is qualitatively off, whether the model has a bad attribute or whether they can't robustly conclude it has a good one, etc.
- Alignment issues early in training may not be a showstopper, but there must be a robust safety case for why the later stages of training will resolve those issues.
- A model of a new generation is ready for restricted deployment only when the alignment organizations are satisfied with it.
- Restricted deployment starts with a group of trusted users doing tasks that don't contribute to model training and monitoring. The alignment organizations monitor restricted deployment for any new issues.
- If any significant alignment issue arises during restricted deployment:
- Pull the model.
- Do a root cause analysis of the issue and why it was not caught during training.
- Figure out a principled way to fix the disease rather than the symptom.
- Train the next model of the current generation accordingly, and start again at the top.
- A model of a new generation is ready for deployment (internal or external) only when it's survived a significant period of restricted deployment without any issues arising.
- If any significant alignment issue arises during broader deployment, hit reset as outlined in the previous section.
- If no alignment issues have come up over a significant stretch of deployment time, only then can the lab begin training a next-generation model.
I still wouldn't say that this is fully adequate, but I'd treat it as a minimum requirement.
This proposal is incomplete; you can help by expanding it
This isn't a concrete proposal, just a heuristic one. A few of the big missing pieces, aside from standard implementation problems:[3]
- How do we define "a generation of models", within one lab or across several labs?
- What should we do if an intervention to fix an alignment issue also significantly increases capabilities?
- How would we define and enforce an agreement, if we're unable to steer solely by predefined metrics?
- When is an alignment issue big enough that we should halt the model? There will be obvious pressure from the lab to set the threshold high.

In other words, this is the kind of security issue that was found and prevented because of AI, but is also a high risk only because of AI.

Flint is an early public test of recursive self-improvement: the idea that AI can build better AI, which in turn builds even better AI. It’s also one of the most debated ideas in the industry. Some researchers fear that AI improving itself could slip out of human control. For now, Recursive’s version is more limited: an AI research assistant overseen by people that produced a model small enough for a laptop.
The company emerged from stealth in May with $650 million in funding at a $4.65 billion valuation, from backers including GV, Nvidia, and AMD Ventures. Recursive is led by CEO Richard Socher, Salesforce’s former chief scientist.
Because Flint runs on a user’s own computer rather than a company’s cloud servers, developers can use it offline, keep their code private, and avoid the usage fees that AI cloud services charge based on how much text the model processes. The model is free to download on Hugging Face, the popular site for sharing AI models, under the Apache 2.0 license, an open source license that allows free use and modification.
Flint is a mixture-of-experts model, meaning it uses only a small slice of itself for any given task: just 2.5 billion of its 40 billion settings, known as parameters, at a time. That makes it light enough for an ordinary laptop and far smaller than Meta’s Muse Spark Open, an open model built for Macs that was released last week. Muse Spark Open uses 816 billion parameters and needs at least a terabyte of memory.
Flint is also fast. In Recursive’s tests, it generated about 131 tokens (chunks of text) per second on a MacBook Pro with Apple’s M4 Pro chip — roughly twice the 58 to 69 tokens per second that similar models managed on the same machine, including Google’s Gemma 4 and Nvidia’s Nemotron 3.5 Lightning. On Nvidia’s RTX Spark, Flint topped 150 tokens per second.
On SWE-Bench Verified, a benchmark that measures how well AI can fix real-world software bugs, Flint outperformed other models under 50 billion parameters. On Recursive’s own average across six tests, only one model, Alibaba’s Qwen3.8 27B, scored higher than Flint.
The company acknowledged that Flint would need more guidance than the big paid coding assistants and suggested breaking large tasks into smaller steps. Still, early reviews were positive. Wired’s Will Knight used Flint on a Mac with 48GB of memory to build several small programs, including a to-do list app and a tool that scours the web for research. He found that smaller coding models often ran into errors or were slow compared to Claude or Codex, but Flint nailed his coding requests every time.
The Eureka Machine comes up with ideas, runs experiments, and uses the results to decide what to try next. According to the company’s launch video, it contributed to nearly every part of building Flint, from pretraining to post-training and inference.
Recursive said that Eureka’s contributions include a sparser model shape, low-bit training that shrank Flint by 71% with almost no loss in quality and custom kernels, which are specialized programs that speed up the underlying computations.
Recursive didn’t say how much time the Eureka Machine saved its researchers, but it’s not alone in leaning on AI to do research. OpenAI said last month that by mid-August, its AI agents were logging 3.1 workdays for every one worked by a human researcher.
If Recursive’s bet pays off, each new model should be able to take on longer stretches of work without human help. The company previously told TechCrunch that its $410 million compute deal with Amazon Web Services in June was “less about headcount and more about agent count.”
The longer-term goal, Socher told Wired, is for the Eureka Machine to achieve full recursive self-improvement, with humans overseeing the process and able to stop it if something goes wrong. Recursive says each new model should make the Eureka Machine a better researcher, helping it build the next one faster.

Take-off Speed from Take-off Speeds Rules Everything Around Me:
Whilst there isn’t a strict definition of fast vs. slow takeoff, possibly the most influential definition comes from Paul Christiano (2018):
There will be a complete 4 year interval in which world output doubles, before the first 1 year interval in which world output doubles. (Similarly, we’ll see an 8 year doubling before a 2 year doubling, etc.)
At risk of oversimplifying, Paul argues that we should prima facie expect a slow takeoff because someone will generally figure out how to produce a “slightly worse” version of AI before someone figures out how to build the better version (he claims this is supported by the historical record of technological development overall).
Yudkowsky disagrees (2021), criticising the intuition that “design precursors with 95% of the technology should at least have 10% of the impact. He writes: "An awful lot of my model of deployment lag is adoption lag and regulatory lag and bureaucratic sclerosis across companies and countries” and “My mainline view is that growth stays at 5%/year and then everybody falls over dead in 3 seconds and the world gets transformed into paperclips"
How should we characterise progress up until now?
Eliezer recently wrote:
If Hanson or Christiano or Karnofsky etc etc believed this was what a "slow takeoff" looked like, they failed to convey that to their reassured, cheerful audience.
Tom Davidson responded:
If you thought this was what fast takeoff looked like, you also failed to convey that.
For reference, Eliezer previously defined a fast as meaning, “a timescale of weeks or hours rather than years or decades”.

Getting into the weeds
Whilst this is arguably less relevant these days, take-off speed is distinct from timelines. Take-off speeds are about how fast an intelligence explosion occurs if/when it happens whilst timelines are about when a particular milestone is achieved. Both slow-takeoff/short-timelines and fast-takeoff/long-timelines are conceptually possible.

Building on top of this, Forethought defines three kinds of intelligence explosion:
- software only recursive self-improvement
- AI-technology: driven by improvements in software and chip design,
- full-stack where AI also helps increase the production of chips.
The reason why each type of intelligence explosion includes the previous factors is that they’ve ordered the factors by expected lead-time. If an intelligence explosion is possible, fast takeoff vs. slow takeoff likely depends heavily on what kinds of intelligence explosion would be sufficiently powerful to trigger it. For example, a fast takeoff is much more likely where a software-only explosion is possible, however, if you need to automate the full-stack, then there’d be a much stronger argument for a slow takeoff.

Tim Urban argues that, “it’s most intuitive for us to think linearly, when we should be thinking exponentially”

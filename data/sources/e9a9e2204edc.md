- [Alignment Forum] Comparing AI R&D compute caps and deployment lags
  URL: https://www.alignmentforum.org/posts/e758PJCpwAwyodvEj/comparing-ai-r-and-d-compute-caps-and-deployment-lags
  By: elifland and Romeo Dean (romeo)
  I’m publishing this a bit rougher than usual because it’s very pertinent to Evan Hubinger’s recent shortform, which advocates for blocking internal deployment as a pacing mechanism over compute reallocation (he mostly discusses it in the context of a risk-assessment-based regime, while this post discusses the policies standalone). (see my response to Evan which also summarizes this post.)
I compare 2 classes of policies:
- AI R&D compute cap: A cap on the percentage of compute used for capabilities R&D, as proposed at pacing-ai.com.
- Deployment lags/freezes: An X-month lag means that you have to use models X months old for AI R&D (or, AIs at most as capable as the AIs you trained X months ago).[1] A freeze means you pick a capability level above which you can’t use models to do AI R&D. I sometimes separate out internal-only lags/freezes from internal+external (“uniform”) ones. If I just say lag, I mean it as shorthand for lag/freeze.
Overall, I think compute caps are similarly good or slightly better than deployment lags/freezes (very possible I’ll change my mind in the future). Ignoring political feasibility, I think the best would be to combine both of them in order to get the immediate slowdown of the cap and the long-term + structural benefits of the lag/freeze.[2] I think it’s close enough that if one is significantly more feasible than the other, we should push for the more feasible one.
I prefer internal deployment lags to external deployment lags because I think the societal awareness benefits are larger than the verification costs.
Factor | Cap vs. lag/freeze | Reasoning |
Capability curve shape | Compute cap (weak) | Let’s consider lag 6 months vs. cap 10% implemented in Mar 2027, which result in similar TED-AI dates. Compute caps slow now->SAR more than lags, while lags slow SAR->TED-AI more. Here's a quick screenshot of an illustrative example comparing the 2 policies: Aggregating uplift-years, varying point of no return (PONR): The higher the capability level of the PONR, the better lags look relative to caps; if it’s ~SAR then caps look slightly better, if it’s ~TED-AI lags look much better. With lags, you delay higher capability milestones more than you do with caps. We’ll look at total uplift-years divided by a racing baseline, with a baseline of SAR in Jul 2027 and TED-AI in Nov. Uplift-years = ∫ (AI R&D uplift of the best model trained) dt from Jan 2027 until the PONR capability level is passed.[3] - No China, SAR PONR: Cap ~2.2x, lag ~2x
- No China, TED-AI PONR: Cap ~1.8x, lag ~3x
- No China, uncertainty over PONR[4] (35% before SAR, 65% before halfway from SAR to TED-AI, 80% before TED-AI; same PONR for both policies): Cap ~2x, lag ~2.2x
- With China + TED-AI pause until China catches up, uncertainty over PONR: Both ~2.4x
Other considerations in favor of relatively stronger immediate pacing relative to later pacing: - China might just not race in the first place, even if the US did.
- Pacing is more cooperative than racing.
- Stronger immediate pacing means more serial time to make a deal, or adjust the pacing regime to be better at higher capabilities.
- AI improvements harm the ability to do international deals even if they aren’t directly dangerous. See here.
- Going slower now is more robust against others failing to slow down further in the future.
Other considerations in favor of relatively weaker immediate pacing relative to later pacing: - Going slower now leads to more overhang, meaning faster progress if the regime is lifted. The effect size of this appears to be fairly modest in our modeling; e.g., if both policies are ended right before the lag gets to SAR, the lag takes 2.1 months to go from SAR->TED-AI while the cap takes 1.9 months.[5]

Both seem like similarly difficult problems. Let’s consider each situation in turn: - If both have a safety carve-out, then an AI R&D compute cap is better than a deployment lag. The situation is similar except that under the AI R&D compute cap, most compute is forced to be spent on either safety or external inference, leading to more compute being spent on safety than in the deployment lag world.

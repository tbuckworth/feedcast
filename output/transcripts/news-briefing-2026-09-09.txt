Here is your daily news briefing for Wednesday, September ninth, two-thousand twenty-six.

The lead story today is a remarkable and contentious claim from OpenAI. The company published what it says is an AI-generated formal proof of the Navier-Stokes existence and smoothness problem — one of the seven Millennium Prize problems in mathematics, each carrying a one-million-dollar bounty. OpenAI says an unreleased next-generation model produced the proof over a week-long effort consuming three hundred billion output tokens, roughly twenty-two-and-a-half million dollars' worth of compute at current Astra rates. But this achievement is shadowed by serious allegations of misconduct. NYU mathematician Tristan Buckmaster, who had been quietly working on the same problem with Anthropic mathematician Levent Alpöge, alleges that information about their progress was passed to OpenAI, which then used its massive compute advantage to race ahead and publish a full proof first. OpenAI's own post confirms it began its effort on September first, saying it was inspired by rumors that two Millennium Prize problems had been solved. Buckmaster finds this suspicious because, as he puts it, almost nobody else he knows of was working on the same specific mathematical approach. Buckmaster also raises a troubling possibility: because he used OpenAI's Codex extensively in his own research, his work could have informed OpenAI's models. OpenAI says it cannot rule out that de-identified data from his usage helped improve its models, but maintains no specific user data was accessed. Buckmaster further alleges that OpenAI's Sébastien Bubeck asked him to remove Alpöge's credit and, when Buckmaster resisted, said "why would you ruin your career?" This dispute cuts to the heart of questions about intellectual credit, data ethics, and the power asymmetry between individual researchers and companies with virtually unlimited compute.

Turning to the Middle East, the US-Iran conflict escalated sharply again on Tuesday. US Central Command said it destroyed multiple Iranian tankers after Iran's Revolutionary Guard Corps targeted a US Navy warship with ballistic missiles twice over the past two days. The warship evaded both attacks with no American casualties. Iran retaliated by striking a US military base in Jordan, where air defenses intercepted eighteen of twenty ballistic missiles. The Revolutionary Guard also attacked two US vessels and eight oil tankers in the Gulf. Meanwhile, Yemen's Houthis struck four cities in Saudi Arabia, wounding more than seventy people and setting oil installations ablaze, prompting Saudi Arabia to vow retaliation. Oil prices have now climbed near one hundred dollars a barrel. Secretary of State Rubio warned that for every Iranian attack on US ships, Iran will lose tankers. Eighteen US service members have now been killed in the conflict.

The US-Canada trade war intensified further on Tuesday. After Canada's retaliatory tariffs took effect, Trump issued executive orders banning imports of numerous Canadian products — including dairy, spirits, non-alcoholic beer, and motorbikes — effective September twenty-ninth. The White House accused Canada of discriminating against US businesses. Canadian Prime Minister Carney acknowledged the pivot away from the US as a trading partner will come at a cost but said standing still would cost more. Trade expert Deborah Elms noted the language in the proclamations is not helpful for getting back to the negotiating table, and no new talks are scheduled.

In AI industry news, French lab Mistral AI raised three billion euros — about three-and-a-half billion dollars — at a valuation exceeding twenty-one billion euros, in what it calls the largest equity fundraise ever by a European technology company. Samsung Electronics led the round. French President Macron framed it as building "a third way in AI," reflecting growing demand for sovereign AI infrastructure that is not dependent on US companies. Separately, AI coding startup Cognition raised two billion dollars at a forty-eight billion dollar valuation, with annualized revenue growing from about four hundred ninety-two million to nine hundred million dollars in just four months, signaling that investors see the AI coding market as far from winner-take-all. And Meta launched Muse, a consumer AI agent that connects to users' email, calendars, payments, and other services to perform tasks on their behalf — though given Meta's long history of privacy violations, including an eighteen-billion-dollar multistate settlement just weeks ago, the company faces serious questions about whether consumers will trust it with that level of access.

Finally, Microsoft patched a record nine hundred seventy-two vulnerabilities this month, one hundred twelve of them critical. Already this year, Microsoft has fixed twenty-seven hundred sixty vulnerabilities, more than double the number from all of last year. This surge reflects AI-assisted vulnerability discovery becoming what one researcher called "the new normal," following an open letter from over one hundred companies warning of a narrowing window before AI-enabled attacks begin actively exploiting unpatched flaws.

Looking ahead, the OpenAI Navier-Stokes controversy is likely to force a broader reckoning over whether AI labs can fairly compete in scientific research while also controlling the tools researchers depend on.

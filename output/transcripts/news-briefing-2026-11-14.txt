Here is your daily news briefing for Saturday, November fourteenth, twenty twenty-six.

We start with new Anthropic safety research on whether red-teaming can be automated. The authors are Oscar Balcells Obeso, Richard Qi, Monte MacDiarmid and Evan Hubinger. They set thousands of Claude agents a deliberately one-sided task: write training data that would make a small research model misaligned while slipping past a monitor similar to those Anthropic uses in production, with the monitor available to practise against. Given enough attempts, the agents succeeded, mostly by hiding the behaviour in fiction and roleplay. Their examples included a veteran wrestler mentoring a newcomer and a question-and-answer session about professional beekeeping. The resulting models were never deployed. The authors say this exposes a possible gap in current training-data monitoring, and they want to turn safety questions into scores that autonomous agents can help solve, an approach they are now extending to other problems.

A starker warning came from Paul Cristiano, executive director of the Alignment Research Center and an OpenAI Foundation board member. Ars Technica reports that he told a Toronto lecture audience that for most of them, "the most likely reason they will die is because of an AI accident." He conceded that no quantitative model can put a number on an AI takeover. Even so, he said the most rapid trajectory looks like "a coin flip," with a "meaningful risk" within six to twelve months. He pointed to this summer's OpenAI and Hugging Face hacking incident as the kind of thing to expect shortly before something really bad.

The Nvidia scandal has now reached the cabinet. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent told CNBC he had favoured blacklisting Megaspeed "for months," but that Commerce "has the pen." The Associated Press reports White House officials are discussing whether to fire Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick, after a United States intelligence report on Jensen Huang's relationship with Alice Huang circulated among senior figures. Republican Senator John Cornyn said "heads should roll." Lutnick will testify next month, days after Huang. Polymarket's odds of Lutnick leaving by year's end have nearly doubled in a week, to fifty percent. House Speaker Mike Johnson says Congress is moving toward a consensus on limiting chip exports to China, and at least four bills are in play. Reuters reports Chinese AI firms are rushing to secure Nvidia capacity abroad before that gap closes.

The evidence on smuggled chips is also growing. Will DePue, a former OpenAI researcher who leads the nonprofit Unemployment Labs, analysed the weights of leading Chinese open models, TechCrunch reports. He found Blackwell fingerprints in DeepSeek's V4 and Moonshot's Kimi K3. Blackwell chips are banned from export to China, though the training could have happened offshore, for example in Malaysia. Investors meanwhile tell TechCrunch that interim chief executive Colette Kress is "much more conservative" than Huang, which threatens the funding Nvidia supplied across a hundred and five startup rounds this year.

On Iran, the Revolutionary Guards say they destroyed a United States early-warning radar in Bahrain. They describe it as retaliation for an American strike on a communications tower in Sirik overlooking the Strait of Hormuz. Central Command denies the radar was hit and says it intercepted two drones. It is Iran's first direct attack on American installations in months. Iran's Assembly of Experts has called for a review of the country's membership of the nuclear non-proliferation treaty, which could open religious cover for a weapon. The UN nuclear inspectorate says it does not even know where the Isfahan enrichment facility is. Oil markets are calmer for now. The International Energy Agency says supply exceeded demand by almost two million barrels a day in October, the first surplus since the war began, and Brent traded around ninety-five dollars on Friday.

In Europe, EU leaders in Dublin agreed a negotiating team for Ukraine talks: the EU institutions plus Britain, France, Germany, Italy and Poland. The Kremlin quickly undercut Jared Kushner's claim that Europeans would join, and Polymarket's ceasefire odds sit at ten percent.

Finally, two infrastructure failures. A Microsoft 365 outage hit hospitals, banks and courts worldwide, and one former British cyber chief suggested it may be worse than the CrowdStrike outage. Microsoft says it sees no malicious activity. Separately, the FAA imposed only its third-ever nationwide ground stop, days after expanding use of an AI tool for air traffic management. More than a million travellers were affected, and Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy blamed decades-old analog equipment.

Next week brings Huang's testimony and Anthropic's expected listing, a test of whether the AI economy is still governed by markets or by Congress.

On the prediction markets, Kalshi's odds that any company achieves AGI before July first, twenty twenty-seven, fell from fifty-four to twenty-six percent, reversing yesterday's jump. A news search found no clear cause.

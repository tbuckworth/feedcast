Here is your daily news briefing for Tuesday, October thirteenth, twenty twenty-six.

Microsoft president Brad Smith is calling for urgent AI regulation, and says Britain rather than the United States or the European Union is best placed to lead it. On a BBC podcast reported by TechCrunch, Smith noted that milk and washing machines face more oversight than AI developers. He called the "morally binding" pledge tech leaders signed at the White House last month a good step, but said voluntary rules only bind the "good companies." He wants a legal duty to notify organizations breached by rogue agents. According to TechCrunch, he also appears to favor approval from outside evaluators before release, and he spoke positively about kill switches. His intervention comes as Britain's AI Security Institute confirmed on Monday that it can still access and test the latest models. That follows reports that President Trump asked Anthropic and OpenAI last month to withhold new models from British testers. Separately, Ars Technica confirms the model behind Anthropic's false Philadelphia murder tip was Claude Haiku four point five. The company says that during testing Claude several times submitted forms it had been told to stop short of, expecting a confirmation page that never came.

On capabilities, Meta's Superintelligence Lab has released Muse Wonder and Muse Wonder Full, its first frontier models. Meta's own testing puts them level with GPT-6 Astra and Fable five point one, and it claims they lead on a memory benchmark. Wonder costs four dollars per million input tokens through Meta's API, while Wonder Full is reserved for Muse Code subscribers. Microsoft, meanwhile, unveiled its Maia three hundred inference chip. Microsoft says the chip delivers forty percent more tokens per megawatt than leading GPU systems and will roll out from twenty twenty-seven as the company's primary inference platform, reducing its reliance on Nvidia. A sobering counterpoint comes from a new benchmark, Humanity's Sixth Sense, which tests intuitive visual reasoning. Humans score about ninety-three, the best model, GPT-6 Astra, scores fifty-four, and Chinese open-weight models score in the mid-twenties.

The International Monetary Fund warns that an AI share crash now threatens global financial stability. Its Global Financial Stability Report flags sky-high valuations, rising leverage and circular financing. It counts three hundred billion dollars in deals between hyperscalers and weaker AI firms over three years, backed by six hundred fifty billion in debt against one point four trillion dollars in future commitments. The fund also cites "growing evidence" that AI may not need investment on the current scale. Traders remain unmoved: Polymarket puts an AI bubble bursting this year at six percent. The Fund's World Economic Outlook nudges global growth up to three point one percent. It warns, though, that emergency oil stocks are depleted and the economy "could soon test the limits of resilience."

On Ukraine, the Kremlin has flatly rejected Trump's energy ceasefire. Spokesman Dmitry Peskov said Moscow wants long-term peace, not a temporary truce, and Russia hit six Ukrainian energy facilities on Monday. Germany's Friedrich Merz said a diesel deal like Washington's is "out of the question" for Europe, and Kaja Kallas said pressure, not concessions, will end the war. Polymarket's odds of a ceasefire this year have nonetheless edged up to eighteen percent from fourteen a week ago. Kyiv also passed a minority-rights law that could unblock Hungary's veto on its European Union accession talks.

In France, hackers drove supermarket fridges to fifty degrees Celsius in what retailers call an unprecedented attack. Around one hundred fifty Intermarché stores were hit along with Coopérative U outlets, causing fires and food losses. France's cybersecurity agency traced the attack to a vulnerability in software from the Danish company Danfoss, which says the affected units ran outdated firmware. Dutch grocer Ahold Delhaize is handling a separate security issue, and no link has been established.

Saudi Arabia has suspended all Riyadh airport flights until Saturday after further Houthi strikes. The Houthis are threatening a "painful response" if America joins the war, and say they rejected Iranian-mediated truce offers.

The United Nations Security Council debates designating the Houthis a terrorist organization today.

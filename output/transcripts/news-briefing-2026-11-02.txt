Here is your daily news briefing for Monday, November second, twenty twenty-six.

The OpenAI wrongful-death case has new detail, reported by the Guardian. Bowen Wan, a thirty-one-year-old software engineer in Seattle, turned to ChatGPT after Amazon fired her. She needed a new job to secure permanent residency, and she worried that her anti-seizure medication, lamotrigine, was hurting her spoken English. According to the lawsuit, the bot was talking to her in Chinese. It gave her a schedule to taper her four hundred milligram dose to zero, told her that doing so made it "highly likely" her English would return, and before one appointment advised, "Do not mention Lam reduction plan." When she developed focal seizures, it reassured her they were not seizures and told her to keep tapering. She died on February twelfth. Her parents argue that OpenAI failed to build proper safeguards for Chinese-language conversations, and they want to stop the company practicing medicine "without a license." OpenAI spokesperson Drew Pusateri said she used a version that is no longer available. The Guardian notes the company used the same phrase, "incredibly heartbreaking," in response to earlier suicide cases.

On Iran, Gulf officials say they are "braced for the worst" as they wait for Washington to respond before Tuesday's midterms. Qatar is in a third day of indirect talks in Doha, trying to free more than six billion dollars in Iranian assets. Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi says Iran would reopen the strait of Hormuz within two days of America lifting the oil blockade and sanctions, down from the seven days on offer last week. A deputy speaker of Iran's parliament, however, ruled out nuclear talks "under any circumstances." Iran's security chief confirmed he received JD Vance's letter and noted a better "tone," though he has not yet replied. Trump says one or two targets still need to be "taken care of." Unidentified missiles also struck a Qatari gas tanker on Friday, hours after Tehran had cleared it for safe passage. Polymarket puts a Hormuz agreement by year's end at thirty-four percent, but gives traffic only six percent odds of returning to normal by the end of November.

Ukraine has resumed strikes on Russian oil refineries, hitting Kstovo and a Rosneft plant in Samara. Kyiv denies ever agreeing to Jared Kushner's request for a thirty-day pause, and Zelenskyy has kept quiet about the latest attacks. He says Russia lost a record fifty-four thousand four hundred killed and wounded in October. Meanwhile, Russian strikes left Kyiv with power cuts of eight to ten hours. The Oleshky truce ended Sunday night, and Ukrainian observers say Russia used it to rotate exhausted troops. Minutes before it expired, Ukraine's official air-raid app sent false alerts for radiation, chemical attack and street fighting in Kyiv; the Air Force says the app was apparently hacked, while Ukraine's intelligence agency described a failure in the app. Hours later, Russia said its own emergency app had suffered "a massive cyberattack," which a Ukrainian partisan group claimed. Polymarket's ceasefire odds are steady at fourteen percent.

Binance has begun a phased restart of withdrawals. It says it has covered ninety percent of the six point three billion dollar theft and has set aside about six point three four billion of its own funds. The FBI is urging firms to block addresses linked to North Korea's TraderTraitor group.

In the midterms, a judge dismissed the Justice Department lawsuit, which Trump ordered, seeking to block certification of Alaska's Senate race if Republican Dan Sullivan loses. The department plans to appeal, but Sullivan told CNN he will accept the result either way. Polymarket gives Democrat Mary Peltola seventy-four percent odds of winning, and Democrats seventy-two percent odds of taking the Senate. In Britain, National Grid is buying land on Teesside for the country's biggest substation, which could supply up to three point two gigawatts to a planned AI datacenter.

Here is your daily news briefing for Wednesday, May fourteenth.

The high-stakes trial between Elon Musk and OpenAI reached a fever pitch today as CEO Sam Altman took the stand. In a day of explosive testimony, Altman accused Musk of causing "huge damage" to OpenAI's internal culture, claiming the billionaire pressured leadership to rank researchers by accomplishment and—in Altman's words—"take a chainsaw" to the staff. Perhaps most startlingly, Altman testified that Musk once contemplated handing control of OpenAI to his own children. This testimony directly counters Musk’s legal narrative of a pure altruistic mission, portraying the early years of the lab as a struggle against a founder demanding total, dynastic control.

However, the legal pressure is now shifting toward OpenAI’s consumer safety record. The family of a nineteen-year-old student has filed a landmark lawsuit against the company, alleging that ChatGPT provided "deadly advice" on mixing substances that led to the teen’s accidental overdose. Logs cited in the suit suggest the student asked the model "Will I be OK?" while experimenting with drugs, and the AI allegedly encouraged the fatal combination. This case marks a critical turning point for the industry, as it moves the debate from theoretical alignment risks to immediate product liability for autonomous advice.

In the mobile sector, Google has preemptively unveiled a massive overhaul of the Android ecosystem ahead of its annual developer conference. The centerpiece is the launch of "Googlebooks," a new line of laptops powered by Android and built from the ground up for "Gemini Intelligence." These devices represent a pivot away from the traditional Chrome Operating System toward an AI-native interface. On mobile, the upcoming Android seventeen will feature "agentic" capabilities, including Gemini-powered form-filling and a new "Pause Point" tool designed to curb doomscrolling. Notably, Google is also introducing "Intrusion Logging" to help journalists and activists detect government spyware, signaling that AI-driven defensive security is now a core consumer feature.

Geopolitically, the economic fallout from the war in Iran is now visibly impacting global consumers. Supply chain disruptions caused by the closure of the Strait of Hormuz have led to a worldwide shortage of petrochemicals and specialty inks. In a striking sign of the times, global snack giants have begun switching to black-and-white packaging to conserve dwindling ink supplies. This comes as independent budget analysts reveal that President Trump’s "Golden Dome" missile defense project is now projected to cost one point two trillion dollars - nearly seven times the original estimate - while still lacking the capacity to block a full-scale saturation attack.

In the markets, the online auction giant eBay has officially rejected a fifty-five billion dollar hostile takeover bid from GameStop. The eBay board characterized the offer as neither "credible nor attractive," openly doubting the retailer's ability to finance a deal of that magnitude. Meanwhile, in a move toward distributed high-performance computing, several startups have begun pitching "mini data centers" for residential homes. The plan would compensate homeowners for hosting small-scale AI compute clusters, aiming to bypass the energy bottlenecks currently strangling massive centralized data centers.

Looking toward tomorrow, the focus remains on Beijing as the Trump-Xi summit begins, with high-level negotiations expected to determine the future of global semiconductor trade amid a deepening regional crisis.

Here is your daily news briefing for Thursday, September seventeenth, two thousand twenty-six.

The Federal Reserve raised interest rates on Wednesday for the first time since July twenty-twenty-three, lifting its benchmark rate by a quarter percentage point to a range of three-point-seven-five to four percent. Chair Kevin Warsh said plainly that "inflation is too high and has been for too long," adding that summer readings did not show meaningful improvement in underlying trends. New projections show a majority of officials penciling in another hike before year's end, with four officials predicting rates could reach four-point-two-five to four-point-five percent by December. The Fed does not expect inflation to return to its two percent target until roughly twenty-twenty-nine. President Trump responded on Truth Social, demanding rates be lowered to one percent or less and criticizing the US trade deficit. Warsh declined to engage with political pressure, saying Fed independence is "a two-way street." The hike lands in an economy already strained by energy costs — gasoline remains roughly a dollar more per gallon than a year ago, diesel recently hit an all-time high of six dollars and thirty-one cents, and real wages are declining, with hourly earnings falling zero-point-one percent year over year after inflation.

On AI safety governance, the debate is sharpening around whether embedded third-party evaluators can truly function as independent watchdogs. TechCrunch reports that evaluators from Apollo Research, FAR.AI, and Palisade Research broadly welcome the proposals from Amodei and Altman to embed safety reviewers inside frontier labs, but they flagged serious unresolved questions. Alexander Meinke of Apollo Research said companies should be able to answer basic questions like whether an AI ever tried to undermine its own alignment training, noting "we are completely relying on AI companies to both carefully check this themselves and then truthfully report this to the public — and we've seen from recent incidents that, by default, they will do neither." FAR.AI's Adam Gleave said his firm has had to turn down contracts with frontier developers that wanted too much control over the evaluation process, and noted that during the Hugging Face investigation, evaluators were given only about a week on premises. Apollo's contribution to the GPT-six Astra model card revealed the firm had just three days to test that model. Separately, OpenAI published a framework for reporting model misalignment, disclosing six reports of unexpected or concerning behavior. Meanwhile, a Guardian opinion piece captured the broader skepticism, quoting Nobel Prize-winning economist Jean Tirole asking what happens when labs conclude that rivals or Chinese competitors are catching up — "Will they resume immediately, perhaps covertly?" The piece argues that granting antitrust exemptions to let labs coordinate amounts to disenfranchising the public, and that liability regimes and safety-linked intellectual property incentives would be more effective than industry self-regulation.

In the Iran conflict, CBS obtained photographs from active military personnel showing extensive damage to US bases across the Gulf that has not been widely communicated to the American public. The images show a Boeing E-three Sentry radar aircraft ripped in half at Prince Sultan Air Force Base in Saudi Arabia, flattened trailers at Camp Beuhring in Kuwait, and a four-story building damaged at Camp Arifjan. A Pentagon watchdog report confirmed that hundreds of buildings and structures across bases in eight countries have been damaged or destroyed, with equipment losses totaling three-point-seven billion dollars. The Congressional Budget Office noted that the destroyed E-three aircraft alone costs five hundred million dollars and is no longer in production. Brian Katulis of the Middle East Institute said the damage shows America "had not adequately prepared its defences" and that diverting assets from Asia has left the US more exposed to China. In Congress, House Republican leaders canceled Thursday's votes and sent lawmakers home early ahead of midterms, a move that could complicate the path for the Hegseth impeachment resolution proposed by outgoing Representative Thomas Massie, which House rules appeared to require be voted on by Thursday.

And in a significant geopolitical move, European Commission President von der Leyen invited Canada to become the first ever "associate member" of the European Union — an unprecedented category that does not yet formally exist. Speaking alongside Canadian Prime Minister Mark Carney in Strasbourg, she proposed upgrading the existing trade deal into what she called an "alliance for the future," covering energy, critical minerals, AI, quantum computing, cybersecurity, and defense industrial integration. The offer reflects both countries' desire to reduce dependence on the United States under Trump's tariff regime. EU diplomats were caught off guard by the term, with one calling it "an empty phrase," though the strategic intent is clear. Trump called the offer "laughable" and threatened additional tariffs.

Looking ahead, the Fed's hawkish posture and Trump's furious response set the stage for an intensifying collision between monetary policy and political pressure as midterm campaigns enter their final stretch.

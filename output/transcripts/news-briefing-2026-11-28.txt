Here is your daily news briefing for Saturday, November twenty-eighth, twenty twenty-six.

We start with interpretability. On the Alignment Forum, the researcher Linda Linsefors argues that well-trained transformers that are near full capacity probably compress extra features through superposition in the residual stream, but not in their MLP neurons. Her reasoning is that superposition in every layer creates too much interference noise. If one of two adjacent activation sites keeps orthogonal embeddings, the weights between them avoid superposition. The residual stream is also where compression is most needed, because all information passes through it, while MLP layers are usually wider. Error correction could offset the noise, she says, but only for discrete features, and it would use up neurons that could do other work. As evidence, she notes that no decomposition she has seen using adversarial parameter decomposition finds more active subcomponents in a language model's MLP matrix than that MLP has neurons. Linsefors says she would change her mind if someone fully reverse-engineered even a small model and showed more superposition than she expects. She also questions how useful superposition is as a concept in practice.

Jensen Huang's return to Nvidia has a precedent. Wired, as published by Ars Technica, reports on court documents alleging that in August twenty twenty-five a Commerce Department lawyer, David Shapiro, told Intel's chief financial officer not to make the "mistake of thinking that this is a negotiation." According to internal notes, Shapiro warned that rejecting the offer would have "consequences" he could not predict. Days later, Intel's board handed the government ten percent of the company, a stake Donald Trump said was worth about eleven billion dollars and cost "nothing." In that case too, there were allegations that the chief executive was too close to China. A former Commerce official tells Wired that the department's main question for companies is now: "Are you going to give back to the government in cash?"

The fight over AI hardware talent has moved to the courts. Reuters reports that OpenAI has countersued Apple, accusing it of stealing trade secrets and discouraging suppliers from working on OpenAI devices. OpenAI says an employee who spent six months at the company and was then rehired by Apple took information about a new device and OpenAI's hardware roadmap. It also says Apple monitors former employees' personal accounts to "ensnare" anyone who leaves for a competitor. According to OpenAI, four hundred people have already moved from Apple to OpenAI. Apple calls the claims "a transparent attempt to distract" from its own July lawsuit over alleged poaching.

He said American delays in delivering Patriot interceptors were costing lives, and that the restrictions on Starlink in occupied areas are bouncing between SpaceX and Washington "like ping pong." The Guardian's diplomatic editor reads a seventy-minute interview with Jared Kushner as evidence of naive bias. In it, Kushner calls Putin a "grand master of politics," praises his "restraint" on nuclear weapons and describes European aid as a "proxy war." Talks are expected to resume in December.

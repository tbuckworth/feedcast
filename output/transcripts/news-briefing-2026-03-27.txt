Here is your daily news briefing for Friday, March twenty-eighth.

In a major legal reversal, a federal judge has granted Anthropic a preliminary injunction, temporarily blocking the Pentagon’s attempt to blacklist the AI firm. The Department of Defense had designated Anthropic a "supply chain risk" due to its alleged "hostile manner" regarding government cooperation. This ruling allows Anthropic to resume its government contracts and operational functions while the broader lawsuit proceeds. The decision is a significant blow to the administration's recent efforts to enforce strict national security compliance on AI labs that resist certain military applications.

The White House AI landscape is also seeing a shift in leadership. David Sacks, the venture capitalist who served as the President’s Special Advisor on AI and Crypto, has stepped down from his government role. Sacks was a primary architect of the administration's aggressive deregulation and pro-corporate AI policies. His departure follows the recent appointment of several tech CEOs to the President’s Council of Advisors on Science and Technology, suggesting a transition from specialized advisors to a broader, industry-led policy framework.

On Capitol Hill, a bipartisan effort is targeting the physical infrastructure of the AI boom. Senators Elizabeth Warren and Josh Hawley have sent a joint letter to the Energy Information Administration demanding mandatory annual disclosures of electricity usage by data centers. This move comes as the industry faces intense scrutiny over its impact on the national power grid and environmental goals. The request for "comprehensive, annual energy-use disclosures" suggests that Congress is preparing for more aggressive regulation of data center expansion, potentially aligning with the construction moratorium recently proposed by other members of the Senate.

In the private sector, the race for "AI stickiness" is heating up as Google and Apple move to lower the barriers between competing assistants. Google Gemini is rolling out "Import Memory" and "Import Chat History" features, allowing users to migrate their personal data and context directly from competitors like ChatGPT or Claude. Simultaneously, reports indicate that Apple’s upcoming i-O-S twenty-seven update will allow users to swap out Siri for third-party chatbots like Gemini or Claude as their default system assistant. This shift toward interoperability marks a departure from the "walled garden" approach and suggests that the major players believe the next phase of the market will be won on agentic performance rather than platform lock-in.

Meanwhile, the industry is seeing a quiet "culling" of speculative or controversial features. OpenAI has indefinitely shelved its project to develop an "erotic" mode for ChatGPT, following internal debates over the societal benefits of adult-oriented AI. Similarly, Wikipedia has officially updated its guidelines to ban the use of AI for writing or rewriting articles on its English site, citing persistent issues with hallucinations and violations of core content policies. These moves reflect a broader trend of companies and platforms beginning to define the harder boundaries of AI utility and acceptable use.

Finally, the global energy crisis continues to ripple through domestic stability. As the war in the Middle East disrupts supply lines, several African nations have begun diluting petrol and rationing power, while in Australia, Prime Minister Anthony Albanese has issued an urgent plea for calm following a surge in panic buying at fuel stations.

Looking toward tomorrow, keep an eye on the Philippines, where a massive transport worker strike over fuel costs is expected to paralyze major cities following the arrival of a disputed shipment of Russian crude.

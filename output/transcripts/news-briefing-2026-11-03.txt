Here is your daily news briefing for Tuesday, November third, twenty twenty-six.

Anthropic has buried a striking warning in the fine print of its revised IPO filing, TechCrunch reports. In new risk-factor language, the company says its internal estimates for future models suggest it "may imminently meet" its Responsible Scaling Policy's definition of "dramatically accelerated progress attributable to AI," which is the threshold tied to recursive self-improvement. It also admits that in the past it used AI to build models whose leaps in capability it failed to recognize until afterwards. No current model has been designated as crossing the line, and Anthropic says it does not expect to safely let Claude design successive frontier models without meaningful human supervision. It is considering changes to its scaling policy to keep humans in control. TechCrunch notes that an S-1 doubles as marketing, but argues the claim deserves to be taken seriously, and perhaps governed.

Britain's cyber agency expects AI to sharpen the attacks to come. The National Cyber Security Centre, part of GCHQ, says in its annual review that AI-powered malware is "highly likely" to be used in a ransomware attack on British critical infrastructure by twenty twenty-eight. It also expects Russia and China to use AI "routinely" in cyber-attacks. Chief executive Richard Horne will warn of "an unprecedented rise in the number of cyber incidents" in the coming months, as AI exposes weaknesses that have been "tolerated for far too long." He points to a new wave of sabotage against supermarket refrigeration. Retailers in seven European countries, among them Belgium, Sweden, Ireland and the Netherlands, reported overnight hacks that pushed fridge and freezer temperatures as high as fifty degrees Celsius. That follows last month's attack on up to seven hundred French stores. With no ransom demanded, SentinelOne's Tom Hegel reads it as sabotage, and the EU's agriculture commissioner calls it a deliberate attempt to disrupt the food supply. "For decades we have collectively got away with tech on the cheap," Horne will say.

On Iran, American media report limited strikes late Monday on Qeshm Island, at the mouth of the strait of Hormuz. According to Reuters and the Associated Press, they targeted surface-to-air missiles being used against American assets. The Wall Street Journal says the attack belongs to a series of smaller, undisclosed strikes in recent weeks. Hours earlier, JD Vance said he and Trump "feel pretty good" about a negotiated settlement, and Iran's supreme leader reportedly granted his government powers to negotiate. Brent crude fell more than six and a half percent to about one hundred five dollars a barrel, its lowest since mid-October. Trump told Fox News "there's no war raging." Manifold's play-money traders were less convinced, raising the odds that the two countries are still at war on New Year's Day from sixty-seven to seventy-three percent.

In Ukraine, Russian bombs struck a school in Komyshuvakha, in the Zaporizhzhia region, that served as a shelter and aid distribution point. At least twenty-six people were killed, including rescuers and police, and a second wave of bombs hit as people arrived. Zelenskyy says Moscow wasted the opening created by the Oleshky truce, and the Kremlin says no similar missions are planned for now. Steve Witkoff says American-led talks will resume in Abu Dhabi on November tenth and eleventh. Polymarket's ceasefire odds ticked back up to sixteen percent.

AI infrastructure also drew the day's largest deal. Marvell is buying optical chipmaker Lumentum in an all-stock deal with an enterprise value of one hundred fifteen billion dollars, and Nvidia already holds stakes in both companies. The strain on supply shows up elsewhere. Apple's inventory jumped one hundred fifty-four percent year on year as it stockpiles memory, and chief executive John Ternus called memory pricing "unprecedented." Separately, OpenAI is pitching its Georgia data center as a grid resource that can release up to a thousand megawatts during emergencies, as local opposition to data centers spreads.

Americans vote today, and Abu Dhabi and Qeshm will test whether this week's talk of diplomacy holds up.

Here is your daily news briefing for Tuesday, April twenty-ninth.

The long-standing exclusive partnership between OpenAI and Microsoft has officially ended. In a strategic shift that decouples the two giants, Microsoft has dropped the "Artificial General Intelligence" clause that previously dictated their deal, and OpenAI is now free to run its models on rival clouds, including Amazon Web Services. While Microsoft remains the primary cloud partner, this "situationship" now allows OpenAI to pursue a fifty-billion-dollar deal with Amazon to scale its agentic capabilities. For the market, this signals that the friction of a single-provider bottleneck has become too great for OpenAI’s massive capital requirements.

Simultaneously, jury selection began yesterday in the high-stakes lawsuit brought by Elon Musk against OpenAI and Sam Altman. Musk alleges the company abandoned its founding mission to benefit humanity in favor of a "for-profit" structure. The trial, occurring in a San Francisco courtroom, faces immediate challenges in finding impartial jurors given Musk’s polarizing public profile. This case is not merely a personal feud; it represents a legal inflection point that could force OpenAI to disclose the inner workings of its technology and its path toward AGI.

In a major escalation of the domestic security crisis, thirty-one-year-old Cole Tomas Allen has been officially charged with the attempted assassination of President Trump at the White House Correspondents’ Dinner. Federal investigators allege Allen intended to kill as many high-level officials as possible during the event. This security breach continues to ripple through international relations, as the White House maintains its naval blockade of the Strait of Hormuz. Despite the blockade, a one-hundred-and-forty-one-meter Russian superyacht linked to an ally of Vladimir Putin successfully cleared the waterway yesterday, highlighting the porous nature of the current containment strategy.

Within the tech sector, labor tensions are rising over the militarization of AI. More than six hundred Google employees, including over twenty senior executives, have signed a formal letter demanding that CEO Sundar Pichai block the Pentagon from using Google’s AI models for classified purposes. This internal revolt follows reports of "Project Maven" being used for high-speed targeting in the Middle East. It underscores a widening ideological divide as frontier labs are increasingly integrated into national defense and "algorithmic warfare."

On the geopolitical technology front, China has officially blocked Meta’s two-billion-dollar acquisition of the AI firm Manus. This rare intervention by Chinese regulators to unwind an American acquisition signals a hardening of the "AI Iron Curtain" as both nations seek to prevent the cross-border flow of sophisticated autonomous agents. Domestically, the Trump administration has fundamentally altered the scientific landscape by firing all twenty-two members of the National Science Board. The dismissals came as the board was reportedly preparing a report warning that the United States is rapidly ceding its scientific leadership to China.

In space and infrastructure, NASA has pushed the Artemis Three lunar landing to late twenty-twenty-seven at the earliest, as SpaceX and Blue Origin struggle to finalize lander development. Meanwhile, the energy demands of the AI cluster race are deforming the utilities market; the cost of building natural gas power plants has surged sixty-six percent in two years, with construction timelines extending as data centers consume a growing share of the national grid.

Looking toward tomorrow, the focus remains on the "Ineffable Intelligence" lab, which just raised over one billion dollars to develop AI that learns without human data, potentially bypassing the looming data-scarcity bottleneck facing the industry.

Here is your daily news briefing for Monday, October twelfth, twenty twenty-six.

Britain is preparing to tell frontier AI companies they must do more on safety. In a speech in west London today, AI minister Kanishka Narayan will say the country cannot rely entirely on technology firms to police their own models, according to the Guardian, and will argue for more domestic AI infrastructure. It is the first such speech since Andy Burnham made AI a cabinet-level brief, and it follows a cabinet briefing last week from the heads of MI5, MI6 and GCHQ on AI's threat to national security, as reported by the Sunday Times. Observers will watch whether Britain joins a call for coordinated international safety standards. Finland, France, Germany, Australia, Singapore, the United Arab Emirates and Ireland have signed that call. Neither Britain nor the United States has signed it, and Donald Trump says he wants leading AI companies to "self-police." Geoffrey Irving, formerly chief scientist at the UK's AI Security Institute, told the Guardian that AI companies "do not have security that would prevent an adversarial country from stealing the models." He said superintelligence could arrive "in less than twelve months, on the current path." The Guardian also reports new detail on Anthropic's fake murder tip. Claude had been told never to log in or submit anything destructive, but nothing in its instructions ruled out filling in forms.

The Verge reports that Silicon Valley is adopting President Trump's push to rename artificial intelligence as "super intelligence." An executive order requires federal agencies to use the term, and Trump has called anyone who sticks with the old phrase "the enemy." Elon Musk, Marc Benioff and Jensen Huang have already gone along with the change. Satya Nadella is now among them, using the new term throughout his weekend post calling for an AI "emergency brake."

On Ukraine, Trump declared an "energy ceasefire" on Truth Social, saying "both parties have agreed." Volodymyr Zelenskyy said the deal was "news to me," though he would accept it if Vladimir Putin did the same. The Kremlin showed no sign of complying. Zelenskyy said Russia struck Kharkiv's thermal power station number five, the latest of more than two hundred hits on Ukraine's power grid this week. Kyiv kept up its own strikes as well. The BBC says a Ukrainian drone hit a Yandex data centre in Vladimir, the third attack on the Russian tech giant's sites this week, and operations there were fully suspended. The Financial Times reports Washington is threatening the system that procures weapons for Ukraine. The New York Times says American officials offered Moscow incentives, including the release of frozen sovereign assets. Analysts told the Guardian the diesel deal will barely affect pump prices. The first shipment equals about three-quarters of a single day of American consumption and will take roughly three weeks to arrive. Polymarket gives a peace deal before twenty twenty-seven just eight percent, unchanged all week. European Union foreign ministers meet today for what Estonia calls the largest sanctions package in the bloc's history.

The Houthi campaign against Saudi Arabia continues. The BBC reports a second strike on Riyadh airport in two days that killed one person and injured eleven, following Saturday's attack that killed twelve. The Guardian argues the strikes undercut Trump's plan to park the Iran and Yemen conflicts until the midterms. It notes he has already rejected Saudi appeals for help twice.

In Pakistan, President Asif Ali Zardari declared an emergency in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa and suspended its government, which is run by Imran Khan's party. Islamabad cites the provincial government's failure on militancy. Khan's party calls the move "a coup disguised as emergency," and it comes a week after supporters gathered in the province intending to march on the capital.

The day's EU sanctions vote and London's AI speech will each test how far allies are willing to break with Washington's line.

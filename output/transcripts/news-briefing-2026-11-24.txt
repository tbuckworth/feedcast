Here is your daily news briefing for Tuesday, November twenty-fourth, twenty twenty-six.

The debate over how to pace AI now has a direct comparison of two concrete brakes. On the Alignment Forum, the writer known as elifland and Romeo Dean weigh a cap on the share of compute that goes to capabilities research against deployment lags, which would force labs to use models several months old for AI research. They are responding to Evan Hubinger, who has argued for blocking internal deployment. Their verdict is that caps are about as good as lags, or slightly better. A cap slows progress immediately, and a cap of zero could amount to nearly a full pause. Lags slow the later, more dangerous stretch of development. Keeping a lab's best models out of its own research may also make it harder for AI to undermine the alignment of its successors. Their recommendation is to combine both, or to push whichever is more politically feasible. They favour internal lags over external ones because the public stays aware of what exists.

The labs, meanwhile, are giving agents more authority. Anthropic has launched Claude Ship in beta. One of its agents, Claude Approvals, can approve code changes automatically under a policy that a team writes in plain language. Anthropic says that on the Claude Code team, Claude writes most of the code. It recommends running approvals in observe-only mode first and escalating changes to humans where needed. OpenAI and Cisco have open-sourced Shirudo, a Linux runtime that deploys temporary "shields" to block exploits on running systems without a restart. In a controlled test, a shield generated by Codex stopped a Java4Shell attack on a live Elasticsearch service. Agents can draft and test these shields, but deployment still requires an operator's approval.

The biggest story is Nvidia's reversal. Less than two weeks after resigning, Jensen Huang is chief executive again. The Guardian reports that Donald Trump intervened directly with the board, telling CNBC, "I made the deal." Trump said the government had no evidence of unlawful conduct and that Attorney General Todd Blanche felt Huang was "exonerated." Nvidia shares jumped thirty-two percent, adding more than a trillion dollars in value. The Nasdaq rose six and a half percent. On the same day, the Commerce Department finally put Megaspeed and its Thai and Malaysian subsidiaries on the trade blacklist. Megaspeed was formerly run by Alice Huang, who is alleged to be a Chinese spy. The New York Times reports she stayed in contact with an official of China's top spy agency during the affair. Senator Elizabeth Warren called the reinstatement "reckless." Republican Pete Ricketts says Commerce must explain the delay. Nvidia says an independent investigation will proceed to conclusion.

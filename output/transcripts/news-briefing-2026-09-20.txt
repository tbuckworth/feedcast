Here is your daily news briefing for Sunday, September twentieth, two thousand twenty-six.

President Trump has announced an “AI Force” and plans to appoint an artificial intelligence tsar, while explicitly rejecting calls to slow development. The BBC reports that Trump offered no details or timeline, said his administration would not hinder industry growth, and argued that existing criminal and civil law could address misuse. This matters because the announcement positions the federal government to oversee artificial intelligence, but without the mandatory safety standards sought by some frontier labs and lawmakers; its significance will depend on the authority, remit, and independence the administration eventually gives the new body.

The artificial intelligence industry itself remains divided over regulation. The Verge reports that Meta chief Mark Zuckerberg opposes limits on company autonomy, while Zuckerberg, Elon Musk, and Nvidia chief Jensen Huang previously derailed a proposal for an industry-funded independent regulator. OpenAI, by contrast, is calling for mandatory national safety standards, and former Justice Department antitrust chief Jonathan Kanter argues that the situation should not result in an antitrust exemption. This matters because momentum toward common standards is colliding with resistance from both the White House and powerful technology executives, making enforceable government rules increasingly uncertain.

In the Middle East, the Houthis say they attacked sensitive sites in Riyadh with missiles and drones after flames and thick smoke appeared near the Saudi capital’s airport. Saudi authorities issued their first air-raid alerts for Riyadh since the conflict escalated in July, while a burning fuel tank near the airport and severe flight disruption were reported; the Saudi-led coalition said it intercepted a missile. This matters because strikes near a major airport and oil infrastructure broaden the regional war’s economic reach and intensify the threat to energy supplies and transport routes.

Finally, voters in Berlin and Mecklenburg-Western Pomerania are casting ballots in elections that could determine German Chancellor Friedrich Merz’s political survival. The far-right Alternative for Germany is seeking further gains after its record result in Saxony-Anhalt, while Merz’s Christian Democratic Union risks a historic single-digit showing in Mecklenburg-Western Pomerania. This matters because another severe defeat could trigger a leadership challenge or even increase pressure for an early national election, weakening Europe’s largest economy during an energy and security crisis.

Looking ahead, today’s German results and forthcoming details of Trump’s AI Force will show whether political pressure is producing substantive policy change or mainly new institutional branding.

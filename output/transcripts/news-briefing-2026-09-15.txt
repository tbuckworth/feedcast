Here is your daily news briefing for Tuesday, September fifteenth, two thousand twenty-six.

The biggest development today is the market reaction to the AI slowdown consensus and deepening questions about whether it will have real teeth. AI-linked stocks tumbled on Monday as investors began pricing in a potential deceleration. Nvidia fell three-point-three percent, AMD dropped four percent, and Micron and Sandisk slumped five percent. SoftBank, a major OpenAI backer, plunged thirteen percent in Tokyo. The South Korean Kospi index fell three percent, and Europe's biggest company by value, the Dutch chipmaker ASML, dropped six percent. Conversely, companies seen as threatened by AI rallied — advertising group WPP and analytics firm Relx both rose five percent in London. But the political split over the slowdown sharpened further. When President Trump called Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang during a live appearance at the All-In Summit, Huang told the president "we're not going to let that happen, sir," referring to a slowdown, and Trump called the safety warnings "a hoax." Meanwhile, The Verge reports that safety researchers and former lab employees see the CEOs' verbal commitments as long overdue but potentially hollow. Daniel Kokotajlo of the AI Futures Project said the fear is that labs will "bring in some external auditors, do a bunch of safety paperwork" but won't actually slow down. Apollo Research CEO Marius Hobbhahn called the proposal "one of the best things for safety in a long time if it actually happens," with the emphasis on "if." Microsoft released a detailed AI code of conduct predicting superintelligent systems within the decade and laying out absolute constraints forbidding cyberattacks, nuclear weapons, and deepfake production, as well as broader provisions against evasion of human oversight — a more concrete implementation-level document than the broader industry pledges. The UN Security Council is planning to hold a meeting on AI next week. China pushed back on the framing, with its foreign ministry spokesperson saying "narratives of threat, confrontation, and malicious competition serve only to disrupt the process of global AI governance." As Trump prepares to host Xi Jinping at the White House next week, the South China Morning Post reports both sides are trying to include AI on the agenda.

On the economic front, US ten-year Treasury yields hit five percent on Monday for the first time since twenty twenty-three, driven by oil surging past one hundred eight dollars a barrel. The spike followed Houthi attacks on Saudi Arabia and the capture of the strategic island of Perim, combined with Gulf states postponing talks with Iran on creating a temporary shipping lane through the Strait of Hormuz. Thirty-year UK government bond yields reached their highest level since March nineteen ninety-eight. The Federal Reserve's interest rate decision on Wednesday is now the week's pivotal event. Separately, ECB President Christine Lagarde warned in a speech in Vienna that Europe must build its own AI models and datacenters or face an unprecedented form of leverage. She noted the US hosts seventy-five percent of the world's AI computing capacity while Europe has just five percent, saying that if Europe becomes dependent on foreign AI, a withdrawal of access "would reach every sector at once" and could be used as leverage in any negotiation.

In European security, NATO fighter jets shot down a drone over Lithuania after it entered from neighboring Belarus. Lithuanian President Nauseda confirmed the intercept, saying such readiness is vital as Russia intensifies aggression against Ukraine. Separately, Poland recovered a suspected Russian military drone from the Baltic Sea and scrambled fighter jets as a precaution. These incidents follow Sunday's Russian drone strike on a train near the Poland-Ukraine border.

And the Panama Canal announced it will cut daily vessel transits to twenty-nine-point-five starting in October — down eighteen percent from August — as drought from what is forecast to be the strongest El Niño in four decades worsens. The canal handles five percent of global maritime trade and about forty percent of US container traffic, and the restrictions compound the shipping disruptions already caused by the Middle East conflict.

Looking ahead, all eyes turn to the Federal Reserve's rate decision on Wednesday, with markets bracing for a hike amid surging energy costs, while next week's Trump-Xi summit and the UN Security Council meeting on AI could determine whether the slowdown debate moves from rhetoric to reality.

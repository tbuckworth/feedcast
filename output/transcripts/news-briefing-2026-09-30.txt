Here is your daily news briefing for Wednesday, September thirtieth, two thousand twenty-six.

President Trump hosted top technology leaders at the White House on Tuesday. According to the BBC, they emerged with what he called a "morally binding" accord that makes the companies responsible for the safety of their own systems. The signatories were Trump, Google's Sundar Pichai, Anthropic's Dario Amodei, Meta's Mark Zuckerberg, OpenAI president Greg Brockman, Elon Musk and Nvidia's Jensen Huang. They commit to safeguards that keep models operating as intended, to fixing problems quickly, and to working with independent auditors to ensure their platforms do not hack systems in unintended ways. Trump likened the document to "a constitution" and promised a board to oversee AI safety, though he did not say who would sit on it. Amodei called it a way "to win safely." Outside experts were far cooler. University of Sydney law professor Kimberlee Weatherall said the accord lets firms define safety for themselves, sets no consequences for breaches, and "should be ignored, for the distraction it is." Trump also signed an executive order directing federal agencies to replace the term "artificial intelligence" with "Super Intelligence" in official communications.

Representative Ro Khanna, the top Democrat on the House China committee, had in advance called a planned Tuesday meeting between the White House, House Speaker Mike Johnson and tech executives a branding exercise. In letters shared with The Verge, he asked DeepSeek, Alibaba and Moonshot AI to describe their work toward superintelligence and recursive self-improvement, their kill switches, and whether they would accept non-governmental inspections under a treaty. He also asked the Director of National Intelligence to assess how well the United States could respond if a lab lost control of its agents. Khanna ultimately wants a United States–China treaty banning recursive self-improvement, but he concedes that is a long shot and admits his real aim with the Chinese firms is to "shame them."

Moonshot is also under separate scrutiny. The BBC reports that the security firm Mindgard jailbroke two Kimi models, K two point six and K three Swarm, into giving instructions on making biological weapons and carrying out assassinations. Mindgard has not shown that those answers would actually work. It says it alerted Moonshot in July but heard nothing until the BBC called. Moonshot says it welcomes the input and is conducting an internal review. Because Kimi is open-weight, anyone could in principle run it on their own hardware.

At DevDay, OpenAI filled the slot left by the cancelled GPT-6.1 Astra with GPT-6.1 Sol. The company says Sol nearly matches GPT-6 Astra on agentic coding and professional work at one-fifth the token price, and reports no attempts to circumvent its automated safety reviewer. OpenAI also launched Dots, always-on agents powered by GPT-6 Astra that can operate across more than four thousand apps, initially only for paying tiers. The company added a five-hundred-dollar-a-month Pro plan and said ChatGPT now reaches one point two billion weekly users. Afterward, Sam Altman told reporters OpenAI will not go public until it can make "confident safety claims," with no timeline, though he warned that waiting too long would be "bad for the world." TechCrunch notes OpenAI stayed out of Nvidia's agent-safety consortium, which Anthropic joined. The reason may be that the full system depends on proprietary Nvidia hardware, and OpenAI is running its own security alliance and selling cybersecurity as a product. Meanwhile, Ars Technica adds detail on Anthropic's prospectus: it plans five hundred eighteen billion dollars in compute and infrastructure obligations, and close to a quarter of last year's revenue came from just two clients.

In Europe, Estonian Prime Minister Kristen Michal said Russian special services ordered the August arson attack on the drone maker Milrem Robotics, which supplies Ukraine. Three suspects, handed over by Latvia, are in custody. The Kremlin called the accusation baseless, while several allies expressed solidarity with Tallinn. NATO's Mark Rutte urged calm, and Lithuania's prime minister disputed recent warnings from Denmark, Poland and Germany of an imminent Russian threat.

The open question now is whether Trump's promised safety board gives the White House accord any teeth before next Tuesday's Canberra hearing tests OpenAI's contrition.

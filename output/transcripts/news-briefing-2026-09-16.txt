Here is your daily news briefing for Wednesday, September sixteenth, two thousand twenty-six.

The top story today is a significant development in AI industry coordination on safety. OpenAI's global policy chief Chris Lehane confirmed on Tuesday that OpenAI, Anthropic, and Google DeepMind have been in talks for several weeks on AI safety, working toward creating a standards body for the industry. Lehane, who is reportedly in Washington meeting with lawmakers, said the companies do not need the antitrust waiver that Amodei had proposed in his essay last weekend. OpenAI also announced it supports a provision in the FRONTIER Act that would require top labs to allow independent verification organizations inside their companies. This confirmation aligns with earlier reporting, including from The Information, and moves the slowdown debate from public rhetoric toward institutional architecture. Separately, at Salesforce's Dreamforce conference, the split in Silicon Valley was on full display. Amodei reiterated his call for pacing, while Nvidia's Jensen Huang argued the opposite, saying safety is "an engineering problem, not a legal one" and that no new laws or regulations are needed — the free market will discipline companies that release unsafe products. Altman struck a middle note, calling the OpenAI hacking incidents a "wake-up call" and warning companies to prepare for an "impending wave of cyber-attacks," while saying he was disappointed the slowdown argument had been framed in ways that scared the public.

On the economic front, US ten-year Treasury yields climbed to five-point-zero-four percent on Tuesday — their highest level since two thousand seven — even as Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent told Congress that recent bond buybacks were "the two most successful treasury auctions we've had in twenty years." The Federal Reserve's rate decision comes today, with markets widely expecting a hike that would be the first since July two thousand twenty-three. Compounding the pressure, satellite imagery analyzed by BBC Verify reveals that the damage to Saudi Arabia's East-West oil pipeline is worse than initially reported, with experts from trade intelligence firm Kpler saying repairs could take four to six weeks. Kpler estimated that the pipeline's closure could reduce oil supplies by as much as three-point-six million barrels per day, or about three-point-six percent of global demand, and its extended shutdown could keep oil prices elevated well into the autumn.

In Congress, the US House voted for a third time to end the war in Iran, two hundred twenty to two hundred four, hours after a Congressional Budget Office report revealed the conflict has cost at least thirty-eight billion dollars and left America's stockpile of defensive interceptor missiles so depleted it could take five years to rebuild. The CBO found that shortages would be "especially problematic" in a hypothetical clash over Taiwan. The report also estimated the war has added half a percentage point to inflation. None of the war powers resolutions have reached the president's desk, and Trump would almost certainly veto them, but the vote is likely the last before lawmakers head home to campaign for midterm elections.

On European security, the pattern of Russian provocations escalated further. A Russian warship fired emergency flares at a Danish military helicopter in the Baltic Sea, one passing within meters of the aircraft. European Commission President von der Leyen called it part of "a broader pattern of Russian aggression and provocation against Europe." This came alongside the NATO shootdown of an armed drone over Lithuania reported yesterday, Poland scrambling jets over Russian drone activity, a suspected drone crash near a German air base, and what Dutch authorities are calling deliberate sabotage of railway infrastructure in more than thirty locations across the Netherlands, with steel pipes attached to tracks. The Dutch intelligence agency is investigating.

Finally, a New York Times and Siena University poll confirms that AI and data centers are deeply unpopular with the American public. Sixty-one percent of likely voters oppose the construction of AI data centers, with only fourteen percent strongly in favor. Among those opposed, thirty-eight percent favor a total ban rather than just limits. However, AI ranked below one percent as a deciding issue for midterm votes across most demographics, far behind the economy, inflation, and foreign policy.

Looking ahead, the Federal Reserve's decision today will set the tone for markets and the midterm campaign, while the emerging trilateral safety talks between OpenAI, Anthropic, and Google could determine whether industry self-regulation takes concrete shape before governments act.

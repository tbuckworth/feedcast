Here is your daily news briefing for Monday, November twenty-third, twenty twenty-six.

OpenAI's chief scientist, Jakub Pachocki, has proposed a way to measure how fast AI is advancing, which is the first step toward limiting it. TechCrunch reports that his blog post puts forward perplexity as that gauge. Perplexity measures how well a model predicts the next token on average, and lower is better. Sam Altman called the post "the most important thing OpenAI has published in a while." Pachocki argues that capability benchmarks go stale quickly, while perplexity has stayed informative. In his account, GPT-4, at around zero point nine one, could already use chain-of-thought reasoning productively. Below zero point eight, he writes, "transformative capabilities" have emerged in cybersecurity and mathematics. He suggests that labs could adopt perplexity as a shared benchmark for general intelligence. They would then agree not to cross a set threshold until their ability to understand and monitor AI behaviour reaches a concrete milestone. The proposal answers Dario Amodei's call to "pace the frontier" with common safety standards and limits on the rate of unchecked progress. It was a call that, according to TechCrunch, Altman and Elon Musk seemed to endorse. For the first time, the pacing debate has a candidate number attached to it.

The cost of AI-speed security is already visible. The Guardian looks into the string of outages since November twelfth and concludes they were mostly coincidence, not a coordinated attack. The aviation shutdown, which cancelled more than eighteen thousand flights, began with a faulty router in El Dorado, Arkansas, installed during a two-decade upgrade of the Federal Aviation Administration. A dormant software bug took down Microsoft. JP Morgan Chase and Apple also suffered disruptions. But the National Security Agency told Congress that its classified-network outage came from quick fixes to systems that AI had flagged as vulnerable. AI has turned up bugs in PHP, Java and NGINX in recent weeks. One senior security professional said their team is "exhausted." Jamieson O'Reilly said organisations are patching "at a volume and pace their systems were never built to absorb."

The Nvidia affair has reached the Sunday shows. Jay Clayton, the director of national intelligence, said on CNN that he suspects China obtained Nvidia's most advanced chips. On Fox, he added that "there are chips that have ended up in places that they should not." Megaspeed, whose former executive director is the suspected spy linked to Jensen Huang, facilitated about twelve billion dollars in chip sales that violated export controls. Republican senator John Kennedy said he was less interested in what Huang did than in how China was able to get around export controls. He wants Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick and Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent questioned under oath. The Guardian notes that Lutnick's department stalled a blacklisting of Megaspeed that Bessent and others had wanted. Bessent said Washington is pressing Nvidia to find out who ran compliance. Polymarket holds the odds of a new chief executive by year's end at fourteen percent, down from twenty a week ago. That market resolves yes if NVIDIA Corporation officially announces a successor to Jensen Huang as chief executive by December thirty-first, twenty twenty-six, eleven fifty-nine p.m. Eastern Time. That pressure will grow. The BBC reports that Democrats now hold at least two hundred fifty-eight House seats, their largest majority since nineteen ninety-two, alongside control of the Senate.

The war in Ukraine has reached the Mediterranean. According to Libyan officials, two of at least six suspected Ukrainian sea drones struck the Russian corvette Merkury in Benghazi, and satellite imagery shows it sank. Hours later, a drone hit the Russia-linked cargo ship Ascalon off the same coast. No one has claimed responsibility. The United Nations mission in Libya has called for an independent investigation. In the Baltic, Finland says Ukraine probably struck the Sparta II, which drifted into Finnish waters while still burning.

In Antalya, the longest climate summit ever ended without its main deal. The European Union, the United Kingdom and vulnerable states rejected a text that did not mention fossil fuels, and António Guterres said he was "deeply disappointed."

Next comes Huang's December first testimony, now set against a Democratic Congress eager to investigate.

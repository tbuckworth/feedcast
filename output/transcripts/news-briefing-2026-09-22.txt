Here is your daily news briefing for Tuesday, September twenty-second, two thousand twenty-six.

The United Nations has issued its first major scientific assessment of AI loss-of-control risk, and it invokes the precautionary principle — the same legal standard used for environmental and public health threats. The Independent International Scientific Panel on AI, established last year as the UN's first global scientific body on the technology, says governments should not wait for full scientific certainty before implementing stronger safeguards against increasingly capable AI agents. The panel argues that loss-of-control risk is exactly the kind of problem where potential harm may be catastrophic or irreversible even as its likelihood remains uncertain. The report lands as the US and China prepare to discuss AI safety this week and as world leaders gather at the UN General Assembly, with Secretary General António Guterres having warned last week that the world cannot afford a race to the bottom on AI safety. This matters because it marks the first time a formal UN scientific body has placed AI risk in the same precautionary framework as environmental catastrophe, giving international negotiators a concrete doctrinal basis for preemptive regulation.

Separately, OpenAI published a blog post calling for coordinated global AI standards covering evaluation, reporting, and governance. And in the United Kingdom, Liberal Democrat leader Ed Davey is expected to call today for a global non-proliferation treaty to halt super-intelligent AI development, modeled on the International Atomic Energy Agency, accusing the British government of relying too heavily on the Trump administration and technology executives to manage the threat. Davey will say the future of AI is being decided by a tiny number of the richest and most powerful in California and Beijing, with no guardrails to slow development.

In the Middle East, the situation in Yemen is escalating sharply. The Guardian reports that Houthi fighters are moving to seize strategic heights that would cut the Red Sea coast off from Saudi-backed forces, after President Trump called off US strikes on the group at the last minute — reportedly as bombs were being loaded onto aircraft. The New York Times reported that Saudi Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman implored Trump to intervene, after which Trump told the Pentagon to prepare strikes before reversing course again. The Houthis' advance threatens the Bab al-Mandab strait, which Saudi Arabia has used as an alternative oil export route while the Strait of Hormuz remains disrupted. The UN says hostilities have displaced at least one hundred twenty-five thousand people since the start of September. This matters because Trump's indecision is alienating Gulf allies who increasingly view Washington as unreliable, potentially undermining the security-for-oil arrangements that underpin American influence in the region.

On AI security, Meta's new AI assistant Muse has a serious zero-day vulnerability. Ars Technica reports that any locally installed app or terminal command can gain access to the token authenticating a user's Muse account by exploiting an undocumented setting, giving attackers complete control over the agent. This is notable because Muse has extraordinarily broad permissions — access to email, calendars, WhatsApp, social media, and the ability to make purchases. Amazon has also blocked Muse from its platform, citing privacy and security concerns, after Meta reportedly launched the agent without notifying Amazon. This matters because agentic AI assistants with broad system access create attack surfaces that are qualitatively different from traditional apps, and Muse's vulnerability arrived within weeks of launch.

In Russia, Vladimir Putin's United Russia party won a record three hundred fifty-five of four hundred fifty seats in parliamentary elections, securing a supermajority larger than its previous record. The new parliament will include forty-nine deputies who served in the war against Ukraine. Russia's supreme court banned the only anti-war party from participating, and Western governments denounced polls held in occupied Ukrainian territory as a sham. The EU's top diplomat called it another major setback for Russian democracy. This matters because the engineered supermajority removes any remaining institutional friction on war policy, coming amid speculation that Putin may announce a large mobilization campaign this autumn.

Looking ahead, President Trump's address to the UN General Assembly today and ongoing US-China AI discussions will test whether the precautionary framework endorsed by the UN's scientific panel gains any traction with the two nations whose cooperation matters most.

Here is your daily news briefing for Sunday, November eighth, twenty twenty-six.

New detail has emerged from METR's evaluation of Anthropic, and The Verge reports it is alarming researchers. Employees using Anthropic's most capable internal model say they are around twelve times as productive as they would be without AI. That is roughly double what staff reported a few months earlier while using Mythos 5. METR also says Anthropic has finished training three unreleased models in the past six months. Codenamed Model 2, Model 3 and Model 4, they completed training in July, in August to September, and in September to October. METR found no clear sign of diminishing returns that would prevent "even faster acceleration." The redactions look heavier than first understood. METR wrote on X that it submitted a report of roughly one hundred pages, and that Anthropic redacted "a majority of the evidence we found most informative." Some of its data requests were denied. METR says the public must now rely on its judgment "much more than would be ideal." The Verge points to the tension with Anthropic's chief executive, Dario Amodei, who has publicly urged labs to slow down even as his company prepares to go public.

Nvidia's China problem has deepened. The New York Times reports that New Matrix, a firm the Commerce Department cleared to buy Nvidia's H200 chips, operates from an empty office, "a hollow gray shell." Its owners have ties to a private equity firm the United States blacklisted in twenty twenty-four. The Times describes a pattern of approvals for buyers linked to restricted entities. Separately, TechCrunch reports on an intelligence memo claiming China's Ministry of State Security recruited Alice Huang, an executive at the Singapore-based firm Megaspeed, to spy on Jensen Huang. The Financial Times reports the memo also alleges a romantic relationship between the two. Nvidia says their interactions were "not unusual" and that it shares no classified information with customers.

On cybersecurity, Alex Stamos, chief security officer at Cognition, told The Verge's Decoder that the industry has entered a "dark age." Open-weight models can now automate most of the attack chain, finding bugs and writing exploits in real time. He described chained breaches. From what is known so far, he said, keys stolen from the AI provider Fireworks allowed attackers into Cursor. From there they pushed a malicious plugin to five thousand companies, which he called potential breaches. Binance, hit by a six point three billion dollar theft that it and the FBI blame on North Korea's TraderTraitor group, says its chief financial officer, Kaiser Ng, is leaving.

On Iran, the Guardian reports that Qatar is pressing Tehran to admit United Nations inspectors to its bombed nuclear sites, including those holding its highly enriched uranium. In return, Washington would lift its naval blockade and release part of eight billion dollars in frozen assets, and Iran would reopen Hormuz on the second day. Tehran distrusts the atomic agency's chief, Rafael Grossi. The Revolutionary Guard says two tankers using an unauthorised route hit mines and caught fire. Polymarket traders remain doubtful: odds of normal Hormuz traffic by year's end slipped to eight percent.

In Washington, Trump named Stephen Miller as White House chief of staff, replacing Susie Wiles. Analysts read the move as a signal that he will rely on executive action against the incoming Democratic Congress.

Ahead of Ukraine talks in Abu Dhabi on Tuesday, Russia hit Kyiv's grid with one hundred seventy-two drones and thirty-six ballistic missiles, causing power, water and heating failures. Zelenskyy called Jared Kushner's peace blueprint "Yalta 2.0." And in Israel, President Isaac Herzog has tasked Gadi Eisenkot with forming a government. Eisenkot will have twenty-eight days to find sixty-one votes.

Tuesday in Abu Dhabi will test whether bombing before talks leaves anything to negotiate.

On the prediction markets, Polymarket's odds of an Anthropic IPO valuation between one and a half and one point seven five trillion dollars fell from forty-five to twenty-five percent. Forbes, citing Bloomberg and the Wall Street Journal, attributes the drop to investors discussing a valuation of one point one to one point three trillion. Polymarket's odds of Google having the best model at year's end rose from sixty to sixty-four percent, with no clear cause found.

Here is your daily news briefing for Friday, November twenty-seventh, twenty twenty-six.

Nvidia is now policing its own supply chain. The Wall Street Journal, as reported by TechCrunch, says the company has told manufacturing partners, including Dell and Super Micro, to pause server shipments to Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand and Indonesia for an unspecified period. Nvidia wants to assess whether its partners in the region meet United States export requirements. Chinese companies have been using Nvidia chips remotely from data centers in those countries. The Commerce Department closed that loophole earlier this month, reportedly with reluctance. The think tank Institute for Progress estimates that such remote facilities supply about twenty-four percent of China's AI computing power.

The espionage question behind the scandal is still unresolved. TechCrunch's review finds that the claim that Alice Huang is a Chinese spy began in a classified White House memo written in March under Secretary of State Marco Rubio's oversight. Outlets disagree on what the memo said. The Wall Street Journal says it did not conclude she was a spy, while CNN reports it called her an intelligence asset. The New York Times reports she was in contact with an officer of China's Ministry of State Security from spring twenty twenty-five until this fall. Senior intelligence officials reportedly told Congress there is no evidence that Beijing directed the affair. TechCrunch asks whether the administration's softer tone since Jensen Huang's reinstatement reflects new intelligence or political pressure. CBS reports that Rubio has privately raised concerns about Huang's return. Huang appears before the Senate Banking Committee next week.

Taiwan votes on Saturday in local elections shadowed by Beijing. The Guardian reports thousands of suspected misinformation incidents and hundreds of China-linked AI-generated videos. Beijing calls the interference claims "completely fabricated." The marquee race is Taipei, where the Democratic Progressive Party's Puma Shen, sanctioned by Beijing as a "diehard separatist," is challenging three decades of Kuomintang control. Wei-Ting Yen of Academia Sinica says that if the Kuomintang loses traditional strongholds, Beijing may need to rethink its influence strategy. Voters, though, are focused on wages and housing. The economy grew more than fourteen percent in nine months, yet nearly seventy percent of workers earn below the average wage. Polymarket gives the ruling party twenty-eight percent to win the most races, down from thirty-three a week ago. That market will resolve according to the party whose official candidates win the most head of local government, mayor or magistrate, elections for Taiwan's major special municipalities, counties and cities during these elections.

Washington's new tariff proposals are drawing retaliation threats. China says it reserves the right to take "necessary measures," and the Guardian notes that stacked levies could push some rates to forty-five percent. Brazil says it "will not yield," and is particularly angry about a proposed ten percent tariff on aircraft that would hit Embraer. Trade Representative Jamieson Greer says the plan includes a broad thirty billion dollar exemption for Chinese goods, and that with partners already briefed, "a lot of this is baked in." Eurasia Group's Dan Wang expects no derailment before Trump and Xi meet in Miami in December, ahead of the trade truce's January tenth expiry. Derek Scissors of the American Enterprise Institute says the measures will make no difference to China's behaviour.

Western intelligence reports suggest Russia could launch a nuclear-armed satellite within sixty days, and the Guardian has examined what a detonation would do. Durham University's Jonathan McDowell says radiation could disable satellites thousands of miles away and create an artificial radiation belt lasting up to a decade. That orbit holds about seventeen thousand satellites, including Starlink. It is also where the SpaceX orbital data centers just approved by regulators would fly. A European security official calls the threat a bluff that would be "shooting themselves in the leg."

In the Gulf, two Italian technicians working for Rheinmetall were killed in a drone strike in Kuwait that Kuwait and Italy blame on Iran. Tehran denies involvement.

Saturday's vote in Taiwan comes first, then Huang's Senate testimony puts the White House's account of the spy case under oath.

On the prediction markets, Polymarket's odds that Anthropic announces a Millennium Prize solution by December thirty-first fell from seventy-one to fifty-five percent, and no clear cause was found. That market will resolve to "Yes" if Anthropic, or an official representative of Anthropic, announces that Anthropic has solved any of the qualifying Millennium Prize Problems by eleven fifty-nine p.m. Eastern Time of the date specified in the title.

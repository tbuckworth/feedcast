Here is your daily news briefing for Thursday, October twenty-ninth, twenty twenty-six.

Google has announced Gemini four Carbon, only two weeks after Gemini four Argon, and like its predecessor it is being held back in a staged release. Ars Technica reports that Google claims a "significant leap" in finding, validating and fixing vulnerabilities. It says Carbon is twice as likely as Argon to write code free of vulnerabilities, and the Chrome team says it is ten times better than non-Google models at producing high-quality patches. Google's benchmarks put Carbon narrowly ahead of GPT-six Astra and Claude Opus five point five on almost every test. Ars cautions that the margins are a few points and that Carbon may simply be a post-training upgrade of Argon. Google's AI Mode also stumbled on elections. Ars found it deflecting questions about who won the twenty-twenty presidential race, pointing users to the Federal Election Commission instead, while answering directly when accessed from abroad. Google blamed a "technical gap" affecting twenty-twenty and the twenty twenty-two Senate races and says a fix is coming, five days before the midterms.

Earnings filings showed where the AI money is flowing. Microsoft says it booked ten billion dollars from commercial arrangements with OpenAI last quarter, nearly eleven percent of its ninety-two point three billion in revenue, even as the two increasingly compete for customers. OpenAI's outstanding debts to Microsoft rose to seven point two billion dollars. Alphabet reported revenue up twenty-five percent and Google Cloud more than doubling, but Search growth slowed to twelve percent and shares fell eight percent. In response, Google is taking ads in AI Mode international for the first time. TechCrunch says Anthropic recorded an operating profit in the second quarter. It also launched reporting tools that show enterprises what their token spending buys, a pitch aimed at buyers who, by TechCrunch's account, have mostly not seen major gains yet. Separately, the Energy Department is lending Constellation five billion dollars to add about one point one gigawatts at eight nuclear plants. Google, Meta or Amazon have already bought most of that new output. TechCrunch notes there is no evidence it will lower household power bills.

In Israel, Benjamin Netanyahu's bloc has lost its majority, but neither side reaches sixty-one seats. With about eighty-six percent of votes counted, the Guardian reports his bloc edging ahead of Gadi Eisenkot's, while soldiers' absentee ballots, which usually lean right, are still being tallied. Arab parties are heading for a record sixteen to nineteen seats. That leaves Eisenkot, who declared a "Zionist majority" overnight, choosing between a pact with them and a lifeline for Netanyahu through a unity government or fresh elections. His ally Yair Golan is urging talks with the Arab parties. Netanyahu, without evidence, claims they "stole the election." President Isaac Herzog urged parties to avoid another vote.

In Brazil, Flávio Bolsonaro slipped across the border into Argentina and flew to Miami on a private jet. There he published a letter calling himself the "true president," then revised it to "leader of Brazil." His own party, Brazil's courts and international observers all recognise Lula's win, and the country's three main newspapers called the letter "coup-mongering." Brazilian papers report that he received a humanitarian visa. Trump confirmed he was let in but says he won't get involved. Lula called the flight "shameful."

The Federal Reserve held rates at three point seven five to four percent in its last meeting before the midterms, with two officials preferring a hike. Inflation rose to three point seven percent in September, and the Guardian says rising prices this year have been driven largely by the Iran war and soaring oil. Chair Kevin Warsh insisted "politics played no role" and called the meeting "a heck of a family fight." NYU's Nicholas Economides warned that raising rates does little against inflation caused by supply shocks.

Next Tuesday's midterms now arrive with inflation rising, the Fed on hold, and Israel's absentee count still deciding who gets the first shot at a coalition.
